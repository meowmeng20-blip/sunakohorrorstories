PONTIANAK

Sender: Dimas Prasetyo • Southeast Asian Folklore

My name is Dimas. I am thirty-four years old, born and raised in the coastal city of Makassar, South Sulawesi. I work as a field surveyor and heavy machinery logistics consultant for an Indonesian infrastructure firm. Over the past decade, my job has taken me to some of the most remote corners of Maritime Southeast Asia—from the dense jungle heartlands of East Kalimantan to the isolated mountain passes of West Papua.

I’m a man who lives by blueprints, satellite maps, and soil tests. I’ve never believed in superstitions, shamanic tales, or forest folklore. In my line of work, if something bumps in the night, it’s usually a wild boar, a sun bear, or an unstable timber rack.

At least, that was what I believed until November of 2024, when I was assigned to oversee a cross-border road construction survey along the rugged boundary between West Kalimantan and Sarawak, Malaysia.

The project involved carving out a secondary haul road through a stretch of dense, ancient rainforest near the border town of Lundu in Sarawak. Because the terrain was brutally unforgiving, our crew set up a temporary surveyors' camp deep inside the primary forest—miles away from the nearest paved highway and completely cut off from mobile phone networks.

Our team consisted of four people: myself leading the survey; my junior assistant, a twenty-six-year-old surveyor from Malang named Farhan; our veteran Malay-Malaysian diesel mechanic, Uncle Razali, who had worked in the Borneo timber camps for over thirty years; and a local Iban guide named Dennis, whose ancestors had hunted in those very woods for generations.

The trouble began on our third night in the Lundu forest.

The Warning Signs:

Frangipani & Decay

It was roughly 11:30 PM. The rain had been falling relentlessly since dusk, turning the forest floor into a thick sea of red clay. Farhan and I were sitting inside our main supply tent, huddled around a portable kerosene stove, reviewing our topographical maps while the rain hammered loudly against the heavy blue tarp overhead.

Uncle Razali was outside under a zinc shed, cleaning the mud out of a excavator’s air filter, while Dennis sat on a wooden crate nearby, sharpening his parang (machete).

Suddenly, the smell in the air shifted.

In the rainforest, you get used to the smell of wet earth, rotting vegetation, and diesel smoke. But within seconds, a sudden draft swept through the mesh window of our tent. It carried an overwhelming, almost suffocating aroma of fresh flowers—specifically the intense, sweet fragrance of white frangipani blossom (bunga kamboja). It was so potent it felt as though someone had smashed a bottle of cheap floral perfume directly under my nose.

"Farhan, did you spray something?" I asked, wiping my damp glasses with my shirt sleeve.

Farhan paused, his pen hovering over his logbook. "No, Mas Dimas. Why would I carry perfume into a logging camp?"

Outside, the metallic clatter of Uncle Razali's tools abruptly stopped.

I unzipped the tent flap and stepped out into the humid night. The forest around our clearing was pitch black, illuminated only by the harsh yellow glow of our LED floodlights powered by a diesel generator. The heavy canopy overhead dripped with steady water droplets.

Uncle Razali was standing completely still under the zinc shed, his eyes wide and unblinking, staring into the thick wall of dark rubber trees thirty yards away. Dennis had already stood up, his hand gripping the wooden handle of his machete so tightly his knuckles were white.

"Uncle Razali?" I called out, taking a step toward him. "What's wrong?"

"Shh!" Dennis hissed, snapping his head toward me. "Keep your voice down, Dimas. Don't speak her name, and don't make any sudden moves."

"What are you talking about?" I asked, frowning.

"Look at the smell," Uncle Razali whispered, his voice trembling in a way I had never heard before from a man who had faced wild leopards and armed loggers. "The frangipani... it's too sweet. It's too close."

"It's just wild jasmine or forest blossoms blooming in the rain," I scoffed, trying to sound logical.

"Listen to me, Indonesian," Dennis whispered, stepping closer to me, his voice deadly serious. "In the Malay world, if the frangipani smell is strong, it means she is far away in the deep canopy. But when the flower smell vanishes and is replaced by the smell of rot... she is right next to you."

As if on cue, the sweet scent of frangipani disappeared in an instant.

In its place came a stench so vile, so gut-wrenchingly foul, that my stomach violently convulsed. It was the smell of a bloated, decaying animal carcass mixed with rancid copper blood and wet grave earth. Farhan ran out of the tent, doubled over at the edge of the clearing, and vomited violently into the mud.

Then, the jungle went dead quiet.

Borneo rainforests at night are never silent. They are a deafening wall of sound—cicadas, tree frogs, night birds, and wind rustling through the high canopy. But in a single second, every insect, every frog, and every bird stopped dead. The silence was heavy, suffocating, and unnatural.

Hahahahaha...

The sound came from high up in the canopy of a massive Tapang tree at the edge of our camp.

It was not a normal human laugh. It started as a low, dry, raspy chuckle that sounded like dry leaves being dragged across a concrete floor. Then, it slid upward into a high-pitched, echoing screech that vibrated directly inside my teeth and skull. It sounded filled with an ancient, bottomless sorrow that immediately twisted into pure, unadulterated malice.

"Get inside the generator shed," Uncle Razali croaked, his face gray under his rain-soaked cap. "Now! Do not look up into the trees!"

The Apparition:

The Flying Death Shroud

We didn't argue. All four of us scrambled into the wooden generator shed, slamming the heavy plywood door and dropping the iron latch into place. Farhan was sobbing quietly in the corner, his hands pressed hard over his ears to block out the noise.

Through the small, wire-mesh vent near the roof of the shed, we could look out into the floodlit clearing.

Rustle... rustle... rustle...

High up in the branches of the Tapang tree, sixty feet above the ground, the dense foliage parted.

Something descended. It didn't climb down the trunk, nor did it jump like a monkey or drop like an animal. It drifted downward through the heavy rain, slow and weightless, like a discarded bedsheet floating in still water.

When it entered the bright yellow beam of our halogen floodlight, my breath caught in my throat, and my blood turned to liquid ice.

It was a woman.

She was dressed in a long, filthy, waterlogged burial shroud of white cloth—the Malay kain kafan—that dragged past her feet, soaked in dark mud and yellow fluid around the hem. Her hair was impossibly long, jet-black, matted with rotting leaves, and reached all the way down to her knees.

She turned her head toward our shed with a slow, mechanical jerk.

Her face was not human. Her skin was taut, pale as a scraped bone, and covered in dark, weeping sores. Her nose was a sunken, hollow cavity, and her mouth was a wide, lipless tear lined with rows of needle-thin, yellowed fangs dripping with dark saliva. Where her eyes should have been, there were only two deep, shadowy sockets filled with glowing red embers that seemed to burn through the darkness.

Her fingers were unnaturally long—eight or nine inches each—ending in thick, curved, black talons that scraped against the wet bark of the trees as she moved.

It was a Pontianak—the malevolent spirit of a woman who had died during a violent, tragic childbirth, trapped between the world of the living and the dead, driven by a perpetual, insatiable hunger for the blood and flesh of men.

"Razali..." a voice called out from the darkness outside.

It wasn't a roar or a scream. It was a soft, melodious, hypnotic whisper that seemed to float right through the plywood walls of our shed. It sounded like a young girl calling out to her father.

"Why are you hiding in the dark, Uncle...? Come outside... I am cold in the rain..."

Uncle Razali closed his eyes tight, clutching a small wooden bottle of blessed black seed oil in his trembling hands, desperately reciting the Ayat al-Kursi under his breath.

"Don't answer her," Dennis warned, his voice barely a breath. "If you answer her call, or if you call out her name, she takes that as permission to enter."

Suddenly, a loud THUD slammed against the roof of the generator shed.

The wooden beams above our heads groaned under a heavy weight. The corrugated iron roof buckled downward an inch. Dark, foul-smelling water began dripping through the screw holes, splashing onto the floorboards near my boots.

Scritch... scritch... scritch...

Her long, razor-sharp talons began gouging deep grooves through the iron roofing sheet right above our heads. It sounded like someone dragging giant iron nails across a chalkboard.

"Mas Dimas!" Farhan shrieked, pointing a trembling finger at the wall.

Dark, matted black hair was being shoved through the narrow cracks between the plywood wallboards. Inches of wet, rotting hair slid into the shed like living black snakes, smelling overpoweringly of copper and dead meat.

In a fit of pure, desperate terror, Dennis pulled a small brass vial from his pouch. It contained blessed salt mixed with crushed turmeric and dried lime. He threw the powder directly at the matted hair pushing through the crack.

The moment the powder touched the hair, a deafening, inhuman screech tore through the night—a sound so loud it shattered the glass bulb of our workbench lamp, plunging the interior of the shed into pitch darkness save for the flickering red glow of the generator's indicator light.

A massive impact hit the wooden door. The iron latch bent sideways with a sickening crack.

"HOLD THE DOOR!" I roared, throwing my entire body weight against the wooden planks.

Uncle Razali and Dennis threw themselves beside me, pushing with everything we had. From the other side of the thin plywood, I could feel the intense, unnatural cold radiating off her body. It felt like standing against a slab of solid dry ice. The air passing through the keyhole was freezing, frosting over the brass handle.

"Give me the young one..." her voice rasped, now right up against the wood of the door, no longer soft or sweet, but guttural, deep, and monstrous. "Give me his liver... give me his throat..."

For three long, agonizing hours, we stood shoulder to shoulder in the pitch-black shed, screaming prayers in Arabic, Malay, and Indonesian, holding the door with our bruised shoulders while she circled the shed, beating against the walls, clawing at the roof, and shrieking into the night storm.

Around 4:45 AM, the first faint gray light of dawn began to creep over the eastern horizon.

As the first rooster crowed from a distant Iban longhouse miles away, the freezing draft suddenly vanished. The heavy weight on the roof lifted with a wet rustle.

The sweet smell of frangipani returned briefly for three seconds—and then, the jungle instantly exploded back to life. The cicadas began to buzz, the tree frogs began to call, and the steady hum of rain returned to normal.

She was gone.

We stayed locked inside that shed until 7:00 AM, when the morning sun was high in the sky. When we finally opened the shattered wooden door, the scene outside looked like a warzone.

The heavy zinc roof of the generator shed was shredded, covered in deep, jagged gouges that tore right through the iron sheets. The blue tarp of our main tent was slashed to ribbons.

And right outside the front door of the shed, planted deep in the red mud, were four distinct footprints. They weren't human feet—they were thin, bird-like claw prints, burnt black into the earth as if pressed with red-hot iron.

We didn't pack up the camp. We abandoned thousands of dollars worth of surveying equipment, fuel drums, and tents. We piled into our heavy 4x4 pickup truck and drove at breakneck speed down the muddy trail until we hit the paved road to Kuching.

Farhan resigned from the company forty-eight hours later. He took a train back to his family's home in Malang and swore never to step foot on the island of Borneo again.

Uncle Razali retired from logistics entirely, moving to a quiet village in Kedah.

As for me, I requested an immediate transfer back to our head office in Makassar. I no longer work in field surveying. I sit in an air-conditioned office, reviewing satellite maps on a computer screen, far away from the primary forests.

I’m sharing this story today because most people think the Pontianak is just a fun movie monster, a piece of old Malay folklore used to scare children into coming home before sunset. I know the truth. She is real. She sits high in the dark canopy of the ancient jungle, waiting for unsuspecting men who venture too far past the tree line. If you ever find yourself travelling through the forests of Southeast Asia at night, and the sweet scent of fresh frangipani suddenly fills your car or cabin... do not stop. Do not roll down your window. And whatever you do... never look up into the trees.
