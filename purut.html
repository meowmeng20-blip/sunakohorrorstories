HANTU JERUK PURUT

Sender: Dimas Prasetyo • Indonesian Folklore

My name is Dimas. If you’re reading this, chances are you’ve heard the rumors. People talk about the Jeruk Purut Cemetery in South Jakarta like it’s just another urban legend parents use to scare teenagers into staying home after midnight. I used to think the same thing. I thought it was just ghost stories invented by local security guards to keep kids from smoking weed behind the old Dutch graves.

I was wrong.

Everything changed on a brutally humid Tuesday night last month.

It started innocently enough. My cousin Rio and I were sitting at a roadside warung near Kemang, drinking iced tea and scrolling through our phones around 11:30 PM. Rio is the kind of guy who thinks he’s invincible—he rides a modified Yamaha Mio, laughs at horror movies, and treats every urban legend like a personal challenge.

"Bro," Rio said, slamming his glass down on the wooden table, his eyes bright with a stupid, restless energy. "I'm sick of this boring routine. Let's go to Jeruk Purut."

I nearly choked on my tea. "Are you out of your mind? It’s past midnight. The caretakers lock the gates, and besides, why the hell would anyone want to hang out in a graveyard?"

"That’s the point," Rio grinned, pulling his helmet on. "My friend Indra went there last week with his vlog camera. He swore on his mother's life that he saw the headless priest. He even recorded a weird audio clip of a dog howling like it was dying, even though no stray dogs were around. Come on, Dimas. Don't be a pacak."

That stupid word—pacak, coward—always got under my skin. Twenty minutes later, we were tearing down the quiet, tree-lined streets of South Jakarta, heading straight for Jalan Jeruk Purut.

The Entrance:

Jalan Jeruk Purut

The air grew noticeably heavier the closer we got. The towering banyan trees on both sides of the road formed a dense, natural canopy that blocked out the streetlights, plunging the asphalt into deep, suffocating shadows. Jakarta is a loud city that never truly sleeps, but right outside the cemetery walls, the silence was absolute. It wasn't a peaceful silence; it was heavy, thick, and ringing with an unnatural pressure that made my ears pop.

We pulled up to the side entrance. The main iron gates were padlocked with a heavy iron chain, but a few feet away, a section of the brick perimeter wall had collapsed years ago, leaving a jagged gap wide enough for a person to slip through.

"See?" Rio whispered, killing his motorcycle engine. The sudden drop in sound felt like a physical blow. "The universe is welcoming us."

"This is a terrible idea, Rio," I muttered, my fingers trembling slightly as I pulled out my phone and switched on the flashlight app. The beam cut weakly through the dense fog clinging to the wet grass.

"Quit whining and keep up," Rio muttered, stepping right through the broken wall.

Stepping into Jeruk Purut felt like walking underwater. The ground was uneven, littered with thick roots, rotting leaves, and centuries-old headstones covered in thick, creeping moss. Some graves were massive Dutch colonial tombs with heavy stone slabs; others were simple Indonesian mounds marked with weathered wooden markers. The smell was suffocating—a sickening mix of damp earth, decaying frangipani flowers, and something sharply metallic, like old copper coins left in the rain.

We walked deeper into the labyrinth of graves, our sneakers crunching softly against dry twigs. Every shadow cast by our phone lights seemed to twist and stretch into human shapes.

"Hey, Rio," I whispered, grabbing my cousin's shoulder. "Look at your phone. Do you have service?"

Rio pulled out his phone and frowned. "Zero bars. That’s weird, it was full G five minutes ago. Battery dropped to four percent too. That’s impossible, I charged it to full before we left."

My heart did a violent, painful flip against my ribs. "My battery is dead too. Completely black screen."

Panic, cold and sharp, began to crawl up the back of my neck. In horror stories, that’s always the cue when things go from bad to terrifying.

"Relax," Rio said, though his voice lacked its usual cocky edge. He swallowed hard, looking around nervously. "The batteries probably just overheated. Let’s just walk to the old church ruin in the center and head back out."

We pushed forward, weaving between towering mahogany trees. That was when we heard it.

It wasn't a voice. It was the distinct sound of heavy, deliberate footsteps splashing through mud—squelch, squelch, squelch—moving parallel to us just behind a thick wall of overgrown bushes.

We froze instantly. I grabbed Rio’s arm so hard my fingernails dug into his jacket.

"Did you hear that?" I breathed.

Rio didn't answer. He was staring straight ahead into a clearing near a massive, ancient banyan tree whose aerial roots hung down like thick, woven ropes.

The Encounter:

The Headless Priest

Standing beneath the tree was a tall figure.

At first, I thought it was a security guard doing night rounds. The figure was dressed in an old-fashioned, high-collared white cassock—the kind worn by Catholic priests during the Dutch colonial era. The fabric was stained with yellow age and splattered with dark, dried mud.

"Ehem," Rio called out, his voice cracking slightly. "Good evening, Pak. We're just... taking a shortcut."

The figure didn't turn around. Instead, it slowly raised its right hand.

I will never, for the rest of my life, forget what happened next.

In its hand, the figure was cradling something heavy, round, and wrapped in matted, blood-soaked hair. With a slow, fluid motion, the priest lifted that heavy object up and placed it squarely on top of his neck.

As the head settled into place, the figure slowly turned around to face us.

There was no face. Where eyes, a nose, and a mouth should have been, there was only a smooth, sloping mass of pale, rotting flesh leaking a thick, black fluid that dripped down the front of his white cassock. Deep, hollow gasps echoed from somewhere inside his chest—a sound like air rattling through a wet pipe.

And right beside him, stepping out from the shadows of the banyan roots, was a large black dog. Its eyes glowed a sickly, luminous amber in the dark, and its jaw hung open, dripping thick, yellow saliva onto the grass.

My brain completely short-circuited. Every rational thought vanished, replaced by a primal, screaming terror that paralyzed my legs.

The headless priest took a slow, heavy step forward.

"Run," I choked out, the word tearing through my throat.

We didn't just run; we exploded into motion. I bolted blindly through the maze of graves, tripping over headstones, tearing my skin on thorny bushes, and gasping for air as the thick, humid night air burned my lungs. Behind us, I could hear the heavy, wet footsteps splashing through the mud, closing the distance with terrifying speed, accompanied by the deep, guttural barking of the phantom dog.

I didn't look back. If I looked back, I knew I would see him right behind my shoulder.

We crashed headfirst through the broken brick wall, tumbling violently onto the asphalt of Jalan Jeruk Purut. Rio’s Yamaha Mio was still parked on the shoulder, the keys miraculously still in the ignition.

Rio scrambled onto the seat, his hands shaking so violently he dropped the key twice before managing to jam it into the slot. I leaped onto the back, wrapping my arms around his waist like my life depended on it—because it did.

Rio kicked the starter. The engine sputtered, coughed twice, and then roared to life with a deafening sound that shattered the eerie silence of the night.

As Rio twisted the throttle and we shot down the street, I risked one quick glance back toward the cemetery gate.

Standing right at the edge of the broken wall, illuminated by the pale moonlight, was the tall figure in the white cassock. The head was resting loosely on his shoulders, tilting sideways at an impossible, broken angle. Beside him, the large black dog sat motionless on its haunches, staring directly at us as we vanished down the road.

We didn't stop driving until we reached a brightly lit 24-hour convenience store in Kemang, miles away from Jeruk Purut. We sat on the curb outside, our clothes torn, covered in mud, scratches, and sweat, shaking so hard we couldn't even hold our coffee cups.

When we checked our phones inside the store under the fluorescent lights, something even weirder happened. Both of our batteries instantly jumped from zero back to seventy percent.

And when I opened my camera roll to see if I had accidentally captured anything in the panic, my gallery was completely empty—except for a single, corrupted video file that refused to play. The timestamp on the file said it was recorded at 1:14 AM, from inside the cemetery.

I deleted the file immediately. I threw away the shoes I wore that night. And I swear to God, I will never, ever go anywhere near Jalan Jeruk Purut after dark again. If you think it's just a myth, go ahead and test it out. Walk into those gates alone at midnight. Just make sure you don't ask him where his head is—because once he answers you, he won't stop following you home.
