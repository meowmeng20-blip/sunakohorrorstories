WALDNIEL HOSTERT (ABANDONED HOSPITAL ASYLUM)

Sender: Lukas Weber • European Folklore

Dear Sunako Stories editorial team,

I am submitting this manuscript to your platform because I can no longer carry this alone. For years, my colleagues and I agreed to sign strict non-disclosure agreements regarding what transpired during our municipal survey of the Waldniel Hostert complex in Schwalmtal, Germany. However, after learning that the site continues to draw urban explorers, ghost hunters, and curious teenagers, I feel a moral obligation to record the absolute truth of what happened to us down in those subterranean brick corridors.

I know your audience appreciates eerie narratives, but I must emphasize: I am not a fiction writer. I am a fifty-one-year-old certified electrical systems auditor and heritage conservation specialist based in Munich. Every measurement, date, name, and floor-plan detail listed below is pulled directly from my private journals and surviving diagnostic logs.

If you choose to publish this, please keep my contact details intact for serious researchers. I have not stepped foot in Schwalmtal since November 2018, and I pray I never hear the sound of a brass handbell again for as long as I live.

Section I:

Historical Context & The Mandate

To understand what we encountered, one must first understand the dark history written into the very soil of Schwalmtal-Amern, near the Dutch border in North Rhine-Westphalia.

Constructed in 1913 by the Franciscan Order as a sanctuary and school for physically and mentally disabled individuals, the sprawling complex known as St. Josefsheim Hostert was a masterpiece of Bavarian neo-Gothic architecture. It featured a grand church with twin towers, sprawling three-story residential blocks, high-ceilinged refectories, and an intricate network of underground utility tunnels connecting every building to a central coal-fired boiler house.

However, in 1937, under the rise of the Nazi regime, the Franciscan monks were forced out, and the facility was seized by the state. It was renamed Waldniel Hostert and repurposed as a specialized pediatric clinic under the auspices of the infamous "Child Euthanasia" (Kinder-Euthanasie) program.

Between 1939 and 1945, under the direction of state-appointed physicians, Ward 3 (Kinderfachabteilung) became a site of unspeakable horror. Over ninety children—ranging from infants to young teenagers—were systematically murdered inside those walls. They were not executed with weapons; they were killed slowly through starvation, deliberate exposure to pneumonia, and lethal overdoses of Luminal (phenobarbital) injected directly into their muscles. Their deaths were logged on official state forms as "cardiac failure" or "severe pulmonary infection."

After the war, the complex served briefly as a British Military Hospital (BMH Hostert) until 1991, before being decommissioned, abandoned, and left to decay behind thick iron gates and overgrown pine woods.

In October 2018, a private European heritage trust initiated preliminary discussions with local authorities to assess whether the non-sanctified administrative blocks and outer utility corridors could be safely decontaminated, re-wired, and converted into a historical memorial site.

My engineering firm was contracted to conduct a comprehensive four-night technical evaluation:

  1. Lukas Weber (Myself): Senior electrical systems auditor, tasked with testing high-voltage conduit integrity, grounding grids, and sub-floor cable channels.
  2. Dr. Hannah Vogel: A forty-two-year-old architectural historian and stone preservationist from Cologne, responsible for assessing masonry load-bearing limits and moisture rot.
  3. Maximilian "Max" Schöne: A thirty-two-year-old field technician specializing in infrared thermal mapping, environmental gas sensing, and structural LiDAR scanning.
  4. Dieter Krupke: A sixty-four-year-old retired municipal security guard and lifelong Schwalmtal resident, who possessed the master keys to the perimeter fences and the heavy iron padlock keys for the subterranean coal chutes.

Our work was scheduled to begin on Monday night, October 22, 2018, from 8:00 PM to 3:00 AM each evening.

Section II:

The First Entrance

We arrived at the main wrought-iron gates off Waldnieler Straße at 7:30 PM under a cold, relentless drizzle. The autumn mist hung low over the fields, blanketing the dark spires of the abandoned complex in a thick, grey shroud.

The air smelled heavily of wet pine needles, rotting oak leaves, and damp limestone.

Dieter Krupke was waiting for us beside his utility van, smoking a rolled cigarette. He carried a heavy brass ring containing oversized skeleton keys and an industrial halogen spotlight.

"Lukas, Dr. Vogel, Max," Dieter greeted us in a low, gravelly voice, flicking his cigarette butt onto the muddy gravel. "I’ll tell you before we unlock the doors: the local police don't come in here after sunset. Teenagers break the fence line sometimes, but they never stay longer than ten minutes. If you hear wood scratching behind the brickwork... you do not poke holes in the wall."

Max chuckled, adjusting the strap of his heavy FLIR thermal camera case. "Dieter, damp wood expands and contracts under temperature drops. It creates acoustic resonance in empty rooms."

Dieter looked at Max with hollow, unblinking eyes. "Resonance doesn't ring handbells, young man. And resonance doesn't leave small bare footprints in fresh mud inside a locked room."

We didn't argue. We unloaded our equipment—portable lithium generator packs, high-lumen LED floodlights, laser measurers, and multimeters—and followed Dieter past the overgrown central lawn toward the imposing brick facade of the main administrative block.

The interior of Waldniel Hostert was a hollowed-out shell of grey concrete and peeling yellow paint. Decades of rainwater infiltration had caused the plaster ceiling coats to collapse in many corridors, exposing rusted steel laths and iron piping. Our footsteps echoed with a harsh, sharp slap against the damp terrazzo tile floors.

For the first three hours, our team worked smoothly. Dr. Vogel documented stone degradation in the central chapel, while Max and I traced the primary high-voltage conduits running along the ground floor of Block B.

At 11:15 PM, we prepared to inspect the primary sub-level utility tunnel—a long, vaulted brick passage that stretched over two hundred meters underground, connecting the administrative building directly to the former site of Ward 3 and the old mortuary house.

Section III:

The Sub-Level Conduit

The entrance to the sub-level utility tunnel was located behind a heavy, reinforced steel security door near the former kitchen scullery.

Dieter unlocked the iron latch with a loud, echoic CLANG that rattled down the dark stairwell. The air that rushed up from the basement was instantly different—it wasn't just cold; it was dry, bitter, and smelled powerfully of old chalk, bitter almonds, and stagnant motor oil.

"This tunnel runs straight beneath the old children's ward," Dr. Vogel noted, looking at her yellowed 1941 architectural blueprints. "According to the municipal archives, the heating pipes and electrical lines were routed through here so maintenance staff wouldn't disrupt clinical operations above."

"It also served as a discreet transportation route during wartime winter months," Dieter added grimly, his flashlight beam illuminating the arched red-brick ceiling of the shaft. "When a child died in Ward 3 during the night, they didn't carry the body across the open courtyard in front of the other patients. They brought them down here, through this passage, straight to the ice cellar."

A heavy silence fell over our group. Max cleared his throat, setting his thermal camera tripod on the concrete floor. "Let's stick to the electrical audit, please."

We moved twenty meters into the narrow brick tunnel. Space was tight; high-pressure steam pipes ran along the upper left wall, while rusted iron conduit trays holding heavy copper cables ran along the right.

Max activated his LiDAR scanner. A rotating green laser grid swept across the arched brick walls, projecting a high-definition 3D point-cloud map onto his tablet screen.

At 11:42 PM, Max stopped walking.

"Lukas, take a look at this readout," Max said, frowning at his display. "The LiDAR is picking up a structural void behind the eastern brick wall—about fifteen meters ahead."

"Is it a secondary drainage shaft?" I asked, shining my high-lumen LED torch onto the brickwork.

"No," Dr. Vogel said, stepping closer with her magnifying lens. "Look at the brick bond. This section wasn't built in 1913. This is rough, un-pointed red brick work from the 1940s. Someone walled off a branch corridor after the war."

I pulled out my digital contact multimeter and pressed the grounding probes against the iron conduit tray mounted to the brick.

The digital display on my meter glitched wildly. The numbers scrambled—jumping from 0 Volts to 110 Volts, then dropping to negative values—despite the fact that the entire facility had been physically disconnected from the municipal power grid five hours prior.

"That's impossible," I muttered, re-calibrating the meter terminals. "There is no live voltage entering this site."

Tink... tink... tink...

The sound was faint, echoing from deep inside the brickwork behind the sealed wall segment.

It was the distinct, rhythmic metallic tapping of a small metal object striking a hollow iron pipe.

"Max," I whispered, holding up my hand. "Is your thermal camera picking up anything on that wall segment?"

Max pointed his FLIR camera at the newly bricked-up wall segment.

The thermal screen—which usually displayed cool blues and purples for concrete surfaces—instantly bloomed into an intense, fiery crimson hue. The temperature readout on the screen dropped from 8°C down to -12°C in a matter of five seconds.

"The thermal sensor is reading an extreme temperature drop," Max gasped, his breath visible in the air as frost began forming on the aluminum tripod legs. "Wait... look at the screen shape."

On the thermal display, the solid crimson block began to shift. The heat differential outlined a small, thin human shape sitting on the floor behind the brick wall—a small child with its knees pulled tightly against its chest.

"We are leaving this corridor," Dieter said, his voice dropping to an authoritative whisper. He grabbed Dr. Vogel by the arm. "Now."

Before we could turn back toward the scullery stairs, a sudden, deafening mechanical roar ripped through the underground tunnel.

Section IV:

The Manifestation in Ward 3

It wasn't a voice. It was the sound of a heavy, industrial steam boiler firing up—a sound that hadn't occurred inside Waldniel Hostert since the British army departed in 1991.

High-pressure steam began hissing violently from the rusted iron pipe joints above our heads. White plumes of scalding vapor erupted from hairline fractures in the metal, filling the narrow brick tunnel with a dense, blinding fog that obscured our flashlight beams.

CLANG! CLANG! CLANG!

The iron steam pipes began vibrating so violently inside their wall brackets that flakes of red rust rained down on our jackets like hot embers.

"THE STAIRS! HEAD FOR THE SCULLERY STAIRS!" I shouted, grabbing my diagnostic case and shoving Max forward through the mist.

We sprinted back down the narrow brick shaft, our boots splashing through cold groundwater that had suddenly begun bubbling up from the floor drainage grates.

As we reached the iron security door at the base of the scullery stairs, the door slammed shut with bone-jarring force. The heavy iron latch dropped into place with a loud CLINK.

"IT'S LOCKED FROM THE OUTSIDE!" Max screamed, throwing his shoulder against the heavy steel door. "DIETER! THE KEYS!"

Dieter frantically fumbled with his heavy brass key ring, his shaking hands struggling to fit the skeleton key into the keyhole in the dim, fog-choked light.

Behind us—from the deep, pitch-black length of the steam-filled tunnel we had just fled—a new sound emerged.

It was not steam. It was the frantic, desperate ringing of dozens of small brass handbells—the kind historically placed on pediatric nightstands for sick children to call attending nurses.

Ring-ring-ring... Ring-ring-ring...

The bell ringing was accompanied by the sound of small, bare feet running through the shallow water on the concrete floor—not one pair, but dozens of small feet sprinting through the darkness straight toward us.

"DIETER! HURRY!" Dr. Vogel shrieked, pressing her back against the steel door, her face contorted in sheer terror.

Dieter jammed the heavy key into the lock, turned it with a wet CRACK, and shoved the steel door open.

We tumbled up the concrete stairs, bursting into the ground-floor scullery hall, and slammed the heavy iron door shut behind us. Dieter threw the iron latch into place and wrapped a heavy steel chain twice around the handle, securing it with a padlock.

The moment the padlock clicked shut, the sound of the steam pipes, the running feet, and the handbells stopped dead.

Absolute, suffocating silence returned to the building.

We lay on the wet, peeling floorboards of the scullery, our lungs burning, sweat freezing on our foreheads.

Dr. Vogel was sobbing quietly, her hands pressed over her face. Max sat against the wall, staring at his thermal camera tablet with dilated, bloodshot eyes.

"Max..." I choked out, reaching over and touching his arm. "Is the screen still recording?"

Max didn't speak. He slowly handed me the tablet.

The recorded thermal loop from the sub-level tunnel was saved on the memory card.

In the final five seconds before the steam pipes ruptured, the camera had captured the brick wall fading out under the temperature drop. Standing on the other side of the brickwork—inside the walled-off utility cavity—were eleven small thermal shapes.

They were all standing shoulder to shoulder, their arms outstretched toward the camera lens, their small hands pressed flat against the interior brickwork as if trying to push the wall down from the inside.

Section V:

The Incident in the Courtyard

We knew we should have packed our vehicles and left Schwalmtal right then. But our contract required us to secure the perimeter and lock all master distribution panels before vacating the property, or face severe financial penalties and legal liability from the heritage trust.

At 1:15 AM, after pulling ourselves together, we agreed to conduct a final sweep of the central courtyard to ensure all ground-floor access points were sealed before we departed.

The rain had stopped. A thick, freezing fog had rolled in off the surrounding farmland, reducing visibility in the central courtyard to less than ten feet. The twin dark towers of the old church rose above the mist like jagged black teeth.

In the center of the courtyard sat the overgrown remains of a Victorian stone fountain, now filled with stagnant rainwater, dead leaves, and rotting branches.

As we walked past the fountain toward our utility van, Dr. Vogel suddenly stopped.

"Listen," she whispered.

We froze on the gravel path.

Through the quiet night air came a low, gentle, humming sound. It was a woman’s voice—soft, melodic, and calm—singing a traditional German lullaby (Guten Abend, gut' Nacht).

The singing was coming from the center of the foggy courtyard, right beside the ruined stone fountain.

Dieter raised his industrial halogen spotlight, casting a bright, yellow beam of light through the thick fog.

Standing beside the stone fountain was a figure.

She was dressed in a dark, high-collared nurse’s uniform made of heavy grey wool, complete with a starched white apron and a white nurse’s cap pinned to her hair—the exact uniform worn by state pediatric attendants in the 1940s.

Her back was turned to us. In her right hand, she held a small glass medicine vial; in her left, a heavy iron tray lined with brass syringes.

"Guten Abend..." Dr. Vogel called out, her voice trembling violently. "Wer ist da...?" (Who is there...?)

The figure did not answer. She slowly began to rotate on her heels—not turning her feet, but pivoting smoothly on the damp gravel like a music box figurine.

As her face turned into the beam of Dieter's spotlight, my heart stopped in my chest.

She had no eyes. Her eye sockets were smooth, flat expanses of pale, waxy skin. Her mouth was pinned shut with thick, rusted iron sutures, yet the melodic lullaby continued to emanate clearly from her throat, vibrating directly inside our ears.

Beside her, emerging from the fog around the base of the fountain, were the children.

They were small, emaciated figures dressed in oversized, faded white hospital gowns. Some were sitting in rusted, high-backed iron wheelchairs; others stood on weak, contorted legs, holding small brass handbells in their thin, pale fingers.

Their faces were sunken, their skin grey and bruised, their eyes hollow black voids.

They didn't scream. They didn't move aggressively. They simply lifted their small, thin arms and pointed their fingers directly at us.

"Es ist Zeit für die Medizin..." (It is time for the medicine...)

A dry, raspy voice whispered from the fog behind us—sounding right against the nape of my neck.

"IN THE VAN! NOW!" I screamed, grabbing Dr. Vogel by her coat collar and dragging her back.

We sprinted across the slippery gravel, hurled ourselves into Dieter's utility van, and Dieter slammed his foot onto the accelerator.

The van's tires spun violently in the wet mud, spraying gravel against the brick walls before gripping the pavement. We smashed through the unlocked wrought-iron front gates, tearing them off their rusted hinges, and roared out onto Waldnieler Straße into the night.

Section VI:

The Conclusion & Silent Pact

We drove without stopping until we reached a 24-hour service station on the Autobahn near Düsseldorf.

We sat inside the bright, sterile fluorescent lights of the service station diner for four hours in total silence. None of us drank our coffee. None of us looked at each other.

The next morning, my engineering firm officially terminated our contract with the heritage trust. I filed a formal municipal technical report citing "severe subterranean structural collapse, hazardous chemical contamination (bitter almond/cyanide residues), and extreme atmospheric safety risks" as the reasons for immediate site abandonment.

I paid for Max's damaged LiDAR equipment and thermal sensors out of my personal savings just to ensure no insurance investigator would visit the site to inspect the gear.

Max Schöne resigned from field engineering three weeks later. He moved to southern Austria, sold all his diagnostic equipment, and currently works in software design. He suffers from severe sensory PTSD and cannot enter dark rooms without overhead fluorescent lighting.

Dr. Hannah Vogel took an extended sabbatical from architectural history. She transferred to an administrative university position in Freiburg and has refused to publish or discuss her research on WWII state facilities ever since.

Dieter Krupke passed away in the spring of 2020. Before he died, his daughter contacted me to return a folder of old site maps Dieter had kept in his home. Inside the folder was a handwritten note from Dieter, dated October 23, 2018:

"They are not resting. They are still waiting for the doctors to leave Ward 3."

As for me, I live in Munich. I no longer accept contracts involving historic hospitals, wartime structures, or underground utility vaults.

Local tour operators and urban explorers in Germany still write blog posts about Waldniel Hostert. They post photos of peeling paint, empty hallways, and the ruined church, calling it a "cool abandoned spot" for weekend photography.

They know nothing.

They don't know what is sealed behind the 1940s brickwork in the sub-level tunnel. They don't know who stands beside the fountain when the autumn fog rolls over Schwalmtal.

Waldniel Hostert is not an empty ruin. It is an open wound in history—a dark, frozen monument of suffering that refuses to sleep.

If you ever find yourself driving through North Rhine-Westphalia late at night, and you pass the dark tree line near Schwalmtal... Do not pull over near the old iron gates. Do not go looking for the underground tunnels. And if you ever hear the faint, delicate sound of a brass handbell ringing in the quiet night air... Put your foot on the gas, close your windows, and never look in your rear-view mirror.

Written and submitted by: Lukas Weber

Munich, Germany

(Former Senior Systems Auditor, Munich Heritage Engineering, 2018)
