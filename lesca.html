LES CATACOMBES DE PARIS

Sender: Julian Vance • Forbidden Exploration

My name is Julian Vance. I am thirty-seven years old, born and raised in the damp, cobblestone neighborhoods of Edinburgh, Scotland. For the past twelve years, I have worked as a freelance subsurface cartographer and architectural historian. My profession involves mapping ancient subterranean utility channels, forgotten medieval vaults, and structural foundations across Western Europe.

I am not a ghost hunter. I do not carry EMF meters, dowsing rods, or spirit boards. I rely strictly on handheld LiDAR scanners, infrared thermal cameras, calibrated measuring tapes, and historical blueprints. In my line of work, if you hear an echo or feel a sudden drop in temperature, it is caused by subterranean drafts, hollow limestone pockets, or shifting water tables.

At least, that was my absolute conviction until November of 2025, when I took an off-the-books contract to assist a team of illegal urban explorers—known locally in France as cataphiles—in mapping an unrecorded series of quarry tunnels branching far beyond the legal tourist boundary of the Paris Catacombs.

The Empire of Death:

Six Million Souls Beneath Paris

To understand the sheer magnitude of the Paris Catacombs, you must forget the polished, lit, forty-minute walking loop open to tourists at Place Denfert-Rochereau. Beneath the city of Paris lies a terrifying, pitch-black labyrinth stretching over two hundred miles. Originally carved out during the Roman era as limestone quarries to build the city's grand monuments, these tunnels were repurposed in the late 18th century to solve a crisis: Paris’s church graveyards were overflowing, spilling rotting corpses into cellar walls.

Between 1786 and 1888, the bones of roughly six million human beings were exhumed, transported by black cloth-covered wagons in the dead of night, and stacked systematically into the dark, damp limestone quarries far beneath the city streets.

While the municipal government maintains tight security over the official section, the unofficial tunnels form an underground wilderness. There are hundreds of hidden entry points scattered across Paris—unmarked manholes in abandoned railway yards, forgotten cellars, and storm drain hatches.

My employer for this expedition was a thirty-one-year-old French-Canadian surveyor named Chloe Tremblay, hailing from Montreal. Chloe was an experienced urban explorer who had spent five years cataloging the interdit (forbidden) sectors of the catacombs.

Our team consisted of three people:

  • Myself, handling high-definition LiDAR terrain scanning and spatial mapping.
  • Chloe Tremblay, serving as our team lead and navigator.
  • Jean-Luc Moreau, a veteran French cataphile in his late fifties who had spent thirty years exploring the restricted underground networks.

Our objective was simple: enter through a forgotten maintenance shaft in the 14th Arrondissement, bypass the official barricades, navigate three miles south into a forgotten 18th-century ossuary known in whispers among cataphiles as La Porte de l'Enfer (The Gate to Hell), and capture a complete 3D scan of its structural arches before a municipal concrete-sealing initiative filled it forever.

We met at 1:15 AM on a frigid, rainy Tuesday night near an abandoned rail line along the Petite Ceinture.

We wore heavy rubber hip waders, waterproof coats, tactical helmets fitted with high-lumen headlamps, and carried heavy backpacks loaded with backup battery packs, water, mapping tablets, and three separate mechanical compasses. Jean-Luc carried a worn, hand-drawn map on yellowed parchment—a document passed down through generations of underground explorers.

"Rule number one," Jean-Luc said in his raspy, smoke-roughened French accent, pulling an iron crowbar from his pack. "Once we drop beneath the street level, we are completely on our own. No cellular signal can penetrate sixty feet of solid limestone. If you twist an ankle, no rescue team will find you. If your lamp dies, you are in a darkness so absolute you will go blind in hours."

Chloe nodded solemnly, checking her headlamp strap. "And if we see the chalk mark of the reversed cross or the broken skull... we turn around immediately. Understand, Julian?"

"Understood," I replied, adjusting my LiDAR rig.

We dropped down the narrow, vertical ladder one by one, descending nearly seventy feet into the freezing, humid belly of Paris.

The moment my boots hit the muddy floor of the entry shaft, the noise of the city above—the distant hum of traffic, the rain hitting the asphalt—vanished instantly. It was replaced by an overwhelming, heavy silence, broken only by the steady, rhythmic drip... drip... drip... of groundwater seeping through the limestone ceiling.

The air was dense, cold, and stale, smelling heavily of wet chalk, old lime, stagnant water, and a faint, sweet underlying stench of decayed organic matter that never truly leaves those tunnels.

For the first two hours, our progress was steady. We waded through knee-deep pools of murky gray water, squeezed sideways through claustrophobically narrow stone crevices, and ducked beneath crumbling archways carved by 18th-century quarrymen.

By 3:45 AM, we had left all modern graffiti behind.

We reached a thick iron gate welded shut and reinforced with heavy steel chains—the boundary separating the known cataphile tunnels from the uncharted historical sectors. Jean-Luc produced a pair of heavy bolt cutters from his pack and snipped through the rusted chain links with a sharp, echoing CLANG.

We swung the heavy iron gate open and stepped into the silence beyond.

Immediately, the character of the subterranean world changed. The smooth, modern stone walls gave way to tight, hand-carved limestone corridors neatly stacked on both sides with millions of human bones.

It was breathtaking and harrowing in equal measure. Skulls, femurs, and ribs were stacked six feet high with eerie precision, forming thick walls of human remains. The eye sockets of thousands of skeletons stared blankly into the bright LED beams of our headlamps as we walked past.

"Setting up the first LiDAR station," I said, setting my tripod down on the damp clay floor and initializing the rotating laser scanner. A faint red laser line began sweeping across the stacks of skulls, translating the bone-lined walls into a blue 3D point-cloud on my tablet screen.

"Look at these inscriptions," Chloe whispered, pointing her light at a weathered stone tablet embedded above a bone archway.

ARRÊTE! C'EST ICI L'EMPIRE DE LA MORT
("Stop! This is the Empire of Death")

"We are entering the deep ossuary," Jean-Luc said, checking his wrist compass. He paused, frowning, and tapped the glass dial with his thumb. The steel needle inside was spinning slowly, erratic and unanchored, refusing to point north.

"Electromagnetic interference?" I asked, looking up from my tablet.

"There are no power lines down here, Julian," Jean-Luc muttered softly, his eyes scanning the pitch-black corridor ahead. "The magnetic bedrock in this sector is unstable. Watch your step."

We continued deeper into the maze, turning through a series of identical, suffocatingly tight stone passages.

Around 4:30 AM, the temperature began to drop rapidly. My breath formed thick plumes of white condensation in the air. The water in our hip waders went from cold to bone-chillingly frigid.

Tap... tap... tap...

A faint, rhythmic sound echoed from the dark corridor behind us.

Chloe stopped dead in her tracks, raising a hand. "Did you hear that?"

"It's just water dripping from a ceiling fracture," I said, though my heart took a sudden, heavy leap against my ribs.

"No," Jean-Luc whispered, leaning his head against the damp stone wall to listen. "That's not water. That's boot leather on dry limestone."

We turned off our main headlamps, leaving only a single dim red tactical flashlight on to conserve power and avoid detection by police patrols. We stood frozen in the pitch-black corridor, listening.

Step... step... step...

It was coming from the exact path we had just walked—a steady, unhurried footstep pacing through the dry bone corridors. But what made my blood run cold was the speed. The footsteps were moving far too fast for anyone navigating that jagged, pitch-black labyrinth without a light source. And there was no beam of light visible down the long corridor behind us.

"Who's there?" Chloe called out in French, her voice trembling slightly. "Is someone there?"

Her voice bounced off the thousands of skulls lining the walls, echoing into the darkness: "There... there... there..."

No answer came. Only the sudden, violent stopping of the footsteps.

"We keep moving," Jean-Luc snapped quietly, his hand gripping his crowbar tightly. "We do not wait around to find out."

The Chamber:

La Porte de l'Enfer

We quickened our pace, pushing deeper into the uncharted south sector. The tunnel sloped downward at a steep fifteen-degree incline, plunging us even deeper beneath the surface of Paris.

At 5:10 AM, we reached the entrance of La Porte de l'Enfer.

It was a massive circular chamber carved entirely out of the deep limestone bedrock, roughly forty feet in diameter. In the center of the room sat a deep, dry stone well surrounded by a ring of intricately arranged human skulls. The walls of the chamber were covered in strange, hand-drawn symbols written in dried, dark brown pigment—symbols that predated the French Revolution.

"This is it," Chloe breathed, her flashlight illuminating the massive stone vault. "This chamber isn't in any municipal map."

"Let's get the laser scan done quickly," I said, setting up the tripod in the center of the chamber. My hands were cold and trembling as I calibrated the LiDAR sensor.

As the rotating scanner began its slow, 360-degree sweep, emitting a faint, rhythmic ping... ping... ping..., the batteries on my digital tablet suddenly drained from eighty percent to zero in three seconds. The screen flickered green, glitched violently with static lines, and went completely dead.

At the exact same instant, the high-power LED work lamp mounted on my tripod flickered out.

"What the hell?" I muttered, frantically swapping out the lithium battery pack. "The battery is brand new!"

"Julian..." Chloe’s voice came from the edge of the chamber. It was barely a whisper, choked with absolute terror. "Look at the well."

I snapped my tactical flashlight toward the center of the room.

The dry stone well, which had been completely empty two minutes prior, was now emitting a low, thick, white mist that spilled over the stone lip and pooled across the floor, clinging to our rubber boots like liquid ice.

The smell in the room shifted instantly.

The dry, chalky odor of the limestone vanished, replaced by an overpowering, stomach-churning stench. It smelled like ancient grave dirt, wet hair, open sewage, and copper. It was the sickening, sweet smell of fresh human decomposition.

Hiss...

A long, drawn-out sound emerged from the deep well. It sounded like a dozen lungs exhaling simultaneously through waterlogged throats.

"We leave now!" Jean-Luc yelled, his composure completely shattering. He grabbed Chloe by the arm and shoved her toward the narrow exit archway. "Out! NOW!"

We scrambled toward the exit corridor, but as my flashlight beam swept across the stone doorway, my heart dropped into my stomach.

The archway through which we had entered five minutes ago was gone.

In its place stood a solid, seamless wall of neatly stacked human skulls and long bones—a wall that looked as though it had been resting there undisturbed for two hundred years.

"No, no, no!" Chloe shrieked, throwing her hands against the bone wall, frantically clawing at the skulls. "This was open! We just walked through here!"

"Calm down! Find another exit!" I shouted, trying to keep my voice steady as my breathing turned into ragged, shallow gasps.

Clatter... clatter... clatter...

Behind us, the bone wall surrounding the central well began to vibrate. Skulls began sliding out of their neat stacks, tumbling onto the stone floor and shattering into yellow dust.

From deep inside the dark well, something began to climb up.

It didn't sound like a person climbing a ladder. It sounded like dozens of hard, bony extremities scraping against the smooth limestone walls—a wet, rapid, skittering motion like a giant crab dragging itself out of a hole.

The Living Bones:

The Entity of the Well

"LIGHTS!" Jean-Luc screamed, pointing his light into the well.

Emerging from the darkness of the pit was an entity that defied every law of nature.

It was a towering, contorted mass composed of dozens of pale, emaciated human limbs and bone fragments fused together in an impossible, unnatural geometry. Its skin was translucent, stretched painfully tight over jagged bone, weeping a yellow, foul-smelling fluid. Multiple dark, featureless facial sockets were embedded across its shifting torso, their jaws hanging open in a silent, agonizing, perpetual scream.

It was not a ghost. It was a physical, living manifestation of the six million forgotten souls buried in the dark beneath Paris—an amalgamation of collective grief, death, and ancient subterranean soil.

The entity lifted a long, jointed arm made of fused arm bones and slammed it onto the stone floor with a bone-shattering CRACK.

The air in the chamber turned so cold that the water in my waders began to freeze around my shins. My headlamp flickered violently, illuminating the creature in terrifying, strobe-like flashes as it lunged forward across the room with blinding speed.

"RUN!" Jean-Luc roared, throwing his iron crowbar with all his might directly at the creature's central mass.

The crowbar struck the entity with a wet, heavy thud, but it didn't even slow down.

Jean-Luc grabbed a loose iron beam from a crumbling side support arch and jammed it into a narrow drainage crevice along the far eastern wall of the room—a crevice no wider than two feet, covered in thick cobwebs and dried mud. He threw his weight against the iron bar, levering away a heavy limestone slab to reveal a tight, muddy crawlspace.

"IN HERE! MOVE!" he screamed.

Chloe dove headfirst into the muddy crawlspace. I threw my heavy mapping pack aside, abandoning thousands of dollars worth of LiDAR equipment, and squeezed my shoulders into the tight stone gap right behind her.

"Jean-Luc, come on!" I yelled, reaching my hand back out into the freezing chamber.

Through the narrow crack, I saw Jean-Luc turn around to face the chamber.

The creature was right on top of him.

Its long, bone-fused extremities wrapped around his torso with terrifying strength. Jean-Luc didn't even have time to scream. The sheer force of the impact crushed his chest with a sound like dry branches snapping in a storm.

His headlamp snapped off, plunging his side of the wall into total darkness save for the wet, sickening sound of tearing cloth and breaking bone.

"JEAN-LUC!" I shrieked, trying to crawl back out.

"JULIAN, NO!" Chloe sobbed hysterically from ahead of me, grabbing the collar of my jacket with desperate strength and pulling me forward into the crawlspace. "HE'S GONE! WE HAVE TO CRAWL!"

Driven by pure, unadulterated survival instinct, I turned and clawed my way through the narrow, pitch-black mud tunnel on my stomach. The ceiling was so low that my helmet scraped against the jagged limestone roof with every inch I moved. The air was so tight I could barely expand my lungs to breathe.

Behind us, from inside the chamber, a sound echoed through the stone—a sound that will haunt my nightmares until the day I die.

It was a chorus of hundreds of voices, overlapping, dry, and raspy, whispering in archaic French through the cracks in the rock:

"Restez avec nous... restez dans la terre..."
("Stay with us... stay in the earth...")

We crawled through that wet, suffocating mud pipe for what felt like an eternity, dragging ourselves forward on our elbows, our fingers bleeding as we tore at the sharp rocks. We didn't look back. We didn't stop.

Around 6:45 AM, after nearly two hours of crawling through forgotten drainage pipes, we saw a faint, pale beam of light ahead.

We burst out through a rusted ventilation grate into a muddy storm basin near the Boulevard Saint-Jacques.

We collapsed onto the wet grass beneath the pouring grey Paris rain, sobbing, covered in black mud, raw sewage, and our own blood. The morning sun was just beginning to peak over the rooftops of Paris. The sound of morning commuters, cars honking, and city buses felt surreal—like waking up from a nightmare into a world that shouldn't exist.

We never called the police. How could we? How could two illegal trespassers explain to the French authorities that a veteran explorer was torn apart by a mass of living bones seventy feet beneath the streets of the 14th Arrondissement?

Jean-Luc Moreau was officially reported as a missing person by his family three days later. The official municipal police report concluded that he likely suffered an accidental fall or drowned in an uncharted underwater canal while exploring forbidden sectors alone.

Chloe left France forty-eight hours after that night. She returned to Montreal, deleted all her urban exploration social media accounts, and severed all ties with the cataphile community. Last I heard, she moved to a rural farming town in northern Quebec and refuses to live in any building with a basement.

As for me, I resigned from my subterranean cartography firm the following week. I moved back to Edinburgh, taking a teaching position at a local university. I no longer work underground. I do not map cellars, I do not inspect ancient foundations, and I refuse to enter the underground vaults beneath my own city.

I am writing this account today because people treat the Paris Catacombs as a quirky tourist attraction, a cool photo opportunity, or a fun thrill for urban explorers. Millions of tourists walk down those stone steps every year, smiling as they take selfies next to walls of human skulls. They don't understand what lies beyond the lit pathways. They don't know what happens when you wander too far into the empire of the dead. If you ever visit Paris, stick to the tour route. Do not look for hidden manholes. Do not break through iron gates. And if you ever find yourself in the dark tunnels beneath the city, and you hear footsteps pacing behind you with no light source in sight... Do not turn off your lamp. Do not speak. Just run for the surface before the empire of death claims you too.
