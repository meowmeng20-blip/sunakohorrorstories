OLD CHANGI HOSPITAL

Sender: Kevin Lee • Singaporean Folklore

Dear Sunako Stories team,

I’m submitting this account to your platform because I’ve kept silent about it for nearly thirty years. I know most of your readers tune in for urban legends, creepypastas, and local folklore, but what I am about to write is not a campfire tale. It is a transcript of events that occurred during my brief employment as a junior structural surveyor with the Public Works Department of Singapore in early 1997.

If you publish this, please keep my email address intact for verification, but understand that I no longer reside in Singapore. I left the country in late 1997 and have lived in Perth, Australia ever since. I have never returned to Changi. I never will.

Section I:

The Assignment

In December 1996, the Ministry of Health officially announced that Old Changi Hospital—situated atop Halton Road in Changi Village—would be permanently decommissioned and replaced by the newly constructed Toa Payoh Hospital (later merged into Changi General Hospital in Simei).

Old Changi Hospital had operated for over sixty years. Originally constructed in the 1930s as a British Royal Air Force military hospital, it comprised two primary structures: Block 24 (a multi-story, high-ceilinged concrete complex used for administrative offices and general wards) and Block 37 (a lower, Victorian-style stone and timber building connected to Block 24 by a long, covered steep slope walkway).

During World War II, when Singapore fell to the Imperial Japanese Army in February 1942, the entire hospital complex was seized by the Kempeitai—the Japanese military secret police. They turned the facility into a primary medical base, prisoner-of-war medical depot, and an interrogation center for suspected anti-Japanese operatives.

By January 1997, the hospital had been completely evacuated of patients, staff, and medical furniture. My engineering firm was awarded a short-term municipal contract to perform a comprehensive structural integrity audit, electrical grid isolation, and sub-basement structural mapping of both blocks before the property was officially handed over to the Singapore Land Authority (SLA).

Our field audit team consisted of four people:

  1. Myself (Kevin Lee): Twenty-six years old at the time, serving as the assistant structural auditor and floor-plan draftsman.
  2. Marcus Tan: Thirty-eight years old, our senior site manager and lead structural surveyor. Marcus was a rigid, highly experienced engineer who viewed every building purely through load-bearing calculations, rebar density, and concrete degradation.
  3. Suresh Ramanathan: Thirty-one years old, a senior electrical systems technician tasked with auditing the high-voltage distribution boards and emergency generator conduits beneath the lower block.
  4. Uncle Ah Huat (Loh Ah Huat): Sixty-two years old, the veteran hospital night caretaker who had worked facilities maintenance at Changi Hospital since 1968. He was hired to escort us, carry the master ring of brass keys, and identify sealed utility hatches.

We were scheduled to conduct three overnight inspection shifts starting on Monday, January 13, 1997, from 9:00 PM to 4:00 AM—hours chosen deliberately so our heavy diagnostic equipment wouldn't interfere with daytime perimeter security operations.

Section II:

The First Walkthrough

We arrived at the main gates off Halton Road at 8:45 PM on January 13. The night was oppressive—humid, windless, and smelling heavily of damp soil, dead sea-ferns, and salt air from the nearby Johor Strait.

Old Changi Hospital stood dark atop the hill, its white concrete walls stained with decades of black rain-fungus, rust trails, and peeling latex paint. The sprawling complex looked like a decaying monument rising out of the dense tropical foliage.

Uncle Ah Huat was already waiting at the security guardhouse, chewing on a toothpick while leaning against his ancient Honda motorcycle. He carried a heavy iron flashlight and a ring containing dozens of worn, heavy brass keys.

"Marcus, Kevin, Suresh," Ah Huat nodded curtly as we unloaded our diagnostic cases, LiDAR gear, and portable halogen floodlights from the van. He looked up at the silhouette of Block 24, his eyes squinting in the dim moonlight. "Power is completely cut from the main grid since 6:00 PM today. Only emergency backup lines in the main security room are active. Everything else inside... dark like a tomb. You bring enough batteries?"

"We have six high-lumen tactical lights, battery packs, and portable generator arrays, Ah Huat," Marcus replied briskly, checking his clipboard. "We'll start with Block 24's ground floor, map the elevator shafts, proceed through the covered walkway to Block 37, and finish with the lower utility basements."

Ah Huat spit his toothpick onto the gravel. "Block 24 is fine. Block 37 is fine. But when we go down to the sub-level behind the old mortuary... you follow me close. You don't step inside rooms where the door is chained. You don't take photos of the wall inscriptions. Clear?"

"Ah Huat, we are conducting a government structural audit," Marcus said, chuckling softly. "Not a ghost tour."

Ah Huat didn't answer. He turned, unlocked the heavy iron chain securing the main entrance glass doors of Block 24, and pushed them open.

The air inside the abandoned hospital hit us like a physical wall. It was stale, thick, and freezing—at least five degrees colder than the outdoor tropical night air. It carried a distinct, sickening smell that never quite left my throat: a combination of old floor wax, dried antiseptic disinfectant, damp concrete, and an underlying metallic tang of iron.

Our heavy boots echoed loudly on the terrazzo tile floor as we set up our primary base station in the empty reception hall. The high ceilings amplified every sound—our breathing, the rustle of our rain jackets, the clink of metal tools.

For the first three hours, our work proceeded without incident. Marcus and I used handheld laser measurers to verify wall thickness, column placements, and ceiling joists in the former emergency wards, pharmacy, and X-ray rooms on the lower floors of Block 24. Suresh tested the main circuit breakers, disconnecting old copper wiring runs.

By 12:30 AM, we had completed mapping Block 24 and prepared to move across the long, covered walkway toward Block 37.

Section III:

The Covered Walkway & Block 37

The walkway connecting the two blocks was a steep, sloping corridor lined with concrete pillars and low brick walls, covered by a corrugated metal roof. It descended down the hillside into the denser, darker vegetation separating the upper administrative building from the lower Victorian structure.

As we walked down the corridor, our flashlight beams cut through thick clouds of midges and night moths. The jungle on either side of the walkway was alive with the deafening hum of cicadas and tree frogs.

Then, midway down the walkway, the insect noise stopped.

It didn't fade out gradually; it vanished instantly, as if a switch had been flicked. The ambient noise of the tropical forest was replaced by a dead, unnatural silence so heavy that my ears began to ring.

Suresh stopped in his tracks, shining his flashlight beam into the thick banyan tree roots hanging over the concrete roof. "Did the wind die down?"

"Just keep walking," Ah Huat muttered, his voice unusually sharp. He kept his head down, his iron flashlight pointed firmly at the concrete floor ahead. "Don't look up at the trees."

I looked up anyway.

Hanging from the thick branch of a rubber tree adjacent to the walkway was a shadow. In the dim spill of my flashlight beam, it looked like a long, dark bundle of cloth swaying gently back and forth—despite the fact that there wasn't a single breath of wind in the air. As my light beam centered on it, the shadow shifted slightly, and for a fraction of a second, I smelled an overpowering, sickly-sweet scent of wild frangipani flowers mingled with rotting meat.

I blinked, pulling my gaze away, my pulse hammering in my throat. I didn't say a word to Marcus or Suresh. I hurried my pace, matching Ah Huat's quick steps until we reached the heavy wooden double doors of Block 37.

Block 37 was vastly different from Block 24. Constructed during the British colonial era, it featured high archways, wooden floorboards over concrete slabs, and thick brick masonry. During the Japanese occupation, this block served as a primary field surgery unit and interrogation site.

Ah Huat unlocked the creaking timber doors, and we stepped inside.

The smell here was markedly worse. The damp ocean air had seeped through the wooden window louvers, rotting the floorboards in several corridors. Flakes of green paint hung from the plaster walls like dead skin.

Marcus set up our tripods in the central corridor, calibrating our laser surveying level. "Suresh, go locate the secondary distribution board for Block 37. It should be near the rear service stairs, behind the old isolation rooms."

"I'll go with him," I offered, feeling an overwhelming urge not to be left alone in the long corridors.

"No, Kevin," Marcus countered without looking up from his diagnostic screen. "I need you to sketch the ceiling beam layout in the west corridor while I run the laser baseline. Ah Huat can go with Suresh."

Ah Huat nodded, pulling a smaller key ring from his pocket. "Come, Suresh. The electrical room is down the back stairs near the old holding cells."

They walked down the dark corridor, their flashlight beams fading into the gloom, leaving Marcus and me in the main hallway of Block 37.

Section IV:

The Inscription on the Plaster

I moved into the west corridor with my measuring tape, clipboard, and a high-power tactical torch. The floorboards creaked under my boots with every step.

The rooms along this corridor were small, windowless, and lined with thick concrete walls. They had served as isolation wards during the hospital's operational years, but their architectural design clearly predated modern medical standards. The doors were heavy timber reinforced with iron cross-bars, featuring small, sliding metal view-slits at eye level.

I stepped inside Room 12-B to measure the structural load of the ceiling joists.

As I raised my flashlight toward the wooden ceiling beams, the light beam illuminated the back wall of the cell. The paint here had peeled away entirely, exposing the raw, grey plaster beneath.

Etched directly into the plaster wall were hundreds of deep, jagged tally marks, carved into the hard surface with something sharp—a nail, a piece of wire, or fingernails.

Below the tally marks were several lines of faint, crude text written in Japanese characters (Kanji), along with names written in Romanized English.

I stepped closer, bringing my camera up to document the wall for our historical architectural log. In the lower right corner, near the floorboards, a short sentence was carved deeper than the rest, scratched with frantic, shaky strokes:

THEY DO NOT DIE HERE. THEY ARE KEPT BELOW.

I stared at the scratched letters, a cold prickle of dread creeping up the back of my neck.

Clatter...

A sharp, sudden sound echoed from the corridor outside—the sound of a small metal object dropping onto the wooden floorboards and rolling several feet before coming to a stop.

I snapped my flashlight beam toward the open doorway of Room 12-B. "Marcus?"

No answer.

I stepped out into the hallway. The corridor was completely dark. Marcus was no longer at his surveying station forty feet away. His tripod was still standing, his laser scanner emitting a faint, pulsing red reference line across the wall, but Marcus himself was nowhere in sight.

"Marcus?" I called out, my voice sounding thin and hollow in the vast, empty building.

Roll... roll... stop.

At my feet, resting against the toe of my work boot, was a small, rusted metal cylinder. I bent down and picked it up.

It was an antique brass medical syringe casing—tarnished, heavy, and missing its glass plunger. It was covered in fresh, wet, yellow mud.

Where had it come from? The floorboards beneath me were dry timber.

Before I could process it, a sudden, violent drop in temperature hit the corridor. My breath formed thick plumes of white vapor in the beam of my flashlight. The ambient air went from tropical humidity to a bone-chilling cold in a matter of seconds.

From the far end of the west corridor—from the pitch-black doorway leading down to the basement service stairs—came a sound.

It was a slow, wet, drag-and-step pattern.

Shhhkkk... THUMP.
Shhhkkk... THUMP.

Something heavy was being dragged across the concrete floor of the basement stairwell, coming up toward the wooden corridor where I stood.

I froze, my flashlight beam locked onto the dark stairwell opening sixty feet away.

My mind raced through rational explanations: A stray dog? A local squatter? Marcus carrying a piece of heavy equipment?

"Marcus, is that you?" I shouted, my hand shaking so violently that the flashlight beam danced erratically across the peeling walls.

The dragging sound stopped instantly.

Silence fell over the corridor once again. But this time, the silence was accompanied by an unbearable, sickening stench—the sudden, suffocating odor of unwashed human bodies, gangrene, wet iron, and raw, decomposing flesh.

Then came the whispering.

It didn't come from the dark stairwell. It came from inside the empty isolation rooms lining both sides of the corridor—rooms I had checked five minutes prior.

It was not a single voice. It was a low, dry, raspy chorus of dozens of human voices, whispering in overlapping, frantic fragments of Japanese, English, and Hokkien:

"Tasukete... tasukete..." (Help me... help me...)
"Don't look at the floor... don't look down..."
"Where is the doctor...? Where is the doctor...?"

The wooden floorboards beneath my boots began to vibrate gently, as if dozens of people were pacing rapidly beneath the floor structure.

I didn't wait to see what was coming up the stairs. I turned and sprinted back toward the main hallway of Block 37, my heart hammering like a trapped bird against my ribs.

Section V:

The Separation

I burst into the main hall, almost knocking over Marcus's laser tripod.

Marcus was standing near the front entrance doors, looking at his diagnostic tablet with a deep frown. He turned as I skidded to a halt on the terrazzo floor, my chest heaving, sweat pouring down my neck despite the icy cold air.

"Kevin? What the hell are you screaming for?" Marcus demanded, crossing his arms. "I went to the van to grab replacement survey stakes."

"Marcus... there's someone in the west corridor," I gasped, pointing a trembling finger down the dark hall. "There are people down in the isolation stairwell. I heard them. The temperature dropped—"

"Kevin, pull yourself together," Marcus interrupted sharply, his voice stern. "This building is sixty years old. It sits on a cliff face above the ocean. Temperature drops are caused by cold air drafts coming off the sea through unsealed utility vents. And those sounds are roosting sea birds or wild dogs in the lower crawlspaces. You're an engineer, not a child."

Before I could reply, a high-pitched, deafening electrical shriek echoed through the building's internal speaker system—an old, long-disconnected PA system mounted near the ceiling vaults.

SCREECH...

The speaker horn directly above our heads buzzed violently with static, followed by a dry, crackling voice speaking through heavy radio interference:

"Attention all medical personnel... Ward 4 requires immediate assistance... Ward 4..."

The voice cut off with a loud CLICK.

Marcus froze. He looked up at the rusting metal PA speaker horn, his face going completely blank. "That PA system isn't connected to any live power board. We disconnected the main feed at 6:00 PM."

Before we could digest what had just happened, a frantic shout echoed from the rear service stairwell leading to the basement.

"MARCUS! KEVIN! GET DOWN HERE! HELP!"

It was Suresh's voice. It was choked with sheer, unadulterated terror, followed by the heavy, metallic sound of iron doors slamming shut and a wet, heavy crash.

Marcus dropped his clipboard. "Suresh!"

We drew our heavy tactical flashlights and ran down the central corridor toward the basement stairs.

Section VI:

The Sub-Basement & The Mortuary

The staircase leading to Block 37's sub-basement was carved directly out of solid granite and concrete, descending steeply into the damp belly of the hill. The air down here was thick with the smell of stagnant groundwater, rust, and old oil.

We reached the bottom landing. The sub-basement corridor was narrow, with low concrete ceilings supported by heavy iron beams.

Standing near an open electrical junction box was Uncle Ah Huat. He was leaning against the concrete wall, his iron flashlight dropped on the muddy floor, his face drained of all color. His hands were shaking so hard he couldn't keep his balance.

"Ah Huat!" I yelled, rushing over and grabbing his arm. "Where is Suresh? What happened?!"

Ah Huat pointed a trembling finger down the long, pitch-black utility corridor toward a pair of heavy, rusted double doors locked with a thick iron chain and padlock.

"He... he didn't listen to me," Ah Huat whispered, his voice cracked and breaking. "I told him... I told him not to open the door behind the electrical board. The door to the old operating theater..."

"Where is he, Ah Huat?!" Marcus shouted, grabbing Ah Huat by his shoulders.

"He went inside," Ah Huat choked out, tears welling in his old, bloodshot eyes. "He said he saw a copper grounding wire running inside. He pushed the door open... and then the lights went out. Something... something pulled him in from the dark."

Marcus picked up Ah Huat's heavy iron flashlight. "Stay here with Ah Huat, Kevin. I'm going in."

"No, Marcus, don't!" I pleaded, grabbing his jacket sleeve. "We need to call security—we need to get out of here!"

"Suresh is inside that room, Kevin!" Marcus snapped, shoving my hand away. "I am not leaving a member of my team behind because of an old man's panic attack!"

Marcus marched down the corridor toward the rusted double doors.

The heavy iron chain that held the doors shut had been snapped—not cut with bolt cutters, but twisted and sheared in half, as if subjected to immense, mechanical torque. One of the heavy wooden doors hung open by a few inches, revealing a void of total, absolute darkness beyond.

"Suresh!" Marcus called out, shining his heavy beam through the gap. "Suresh, answer me!"

No answer came from inside.

Marcus placed his boot against the heavy timber door and kicked it wide open.

Our combined flashlight beams flooded the interior of the room.

It was a massive, low-ceilinged chamber carved deep into the hillside bedrock—the old World War II Japanese field operating theater and mortuary prep room.

In the center of the room sat three long, rusted iron operating tables, complete with leather restraint straps, overhead surgical lamp fixtures suspended by chains, and deep floor drainage channels carved into the concrete floorboards. The concrete walls were lined with heavy iron morgue drawers, many of them pulled open, hanging empty like hollow iron mouths.

Standing near the center operating table was Suresh.

He was standing completely rigid, his back turned to us, facing the far wall. His flashlight lay shattered on the floor at his feet, its beam flicking weakly.

"Suresh!" Marcus let out a breath of relief, stepping quickly into the room. "Jesus Christ, man! Why didn't you answer us?!"

Marcus reached out and placed his hand on Suresh's shoulder to turn him around.

The moment Marcus touched him, Suresh collapsed backward like a ragdoll, crashing onto the hard concrete floor.

I let out a strangled gasp, stumbling backward against the doorframe.

Suresh was conscious, his eyes wide, dilated, and bloodshot, staring fixedly at the ceiling in absolute, catatonic terror. He was gasping for air in short, wet, shallow hitches, his throat making a horrific clicking sound.

His face, neck, and forearms were covered in deep, purple, finger-shaped bruises—as if multiple pairs of small, extremely powerful hands had grabbed him and held him pinned against the cold iron table.

"Get him up! Help me get him up!" Marcus yelled, his voice cracking as his rigid, professional composure finally shattered.

I ran forward, and together we grabbed Suresh beneath his arms, dragging his dead weight out of the operating room into the utility corridor.

As we crossed the threshold, the heavy iron morgue drawers along the far wall began to slam shut simultaneously.

CLANG! CLANG! CLANG! CLANG!

The iron doors slammed with deafening force, echoing through the concrete sub-basement like gunfire.

Then, from inside the pitch-black corners of the operating room—from the shadows beneath the iron tables and behind the open mortuary slots—figures began to emerge.

They did not walk. They moved with horrific, unnatural, fluid speed—crawling along the damp concrete floorboards, clinging to the overhead rusted pipes, and sliding across the walls.

They were pale, emaciated human shapes, their bodies contorted into unnatural angles, dressed in the rotting remnants of WWII British military uniforms, Imperial Japanese army tunics, and plain cotton patient gowns.

Their eyes were hollow black sockets, their mouths torn wide open in silent, agonizing screams that emitted no sound—only a high-pitched, brain-numbing vibration that made my nose bleed instantly.

"GET OUT! RUN!" Marcus screamed, completely losing his mind.

He hoisted Suresh over his shoulder with desperate, unnatural strength. I grabbed Uncle Ah Huat by his belt, pulling the terrified old man along as we sprinted back down the narrow sub-basement corridor toward the granite stairs.

Behind us, the sound of hundreds of bare feet, hands, and iron chains dragging across the concrete echoed through the dark corridors, gaining on us with every stride.

Section VII:

The Escape

We scrambled up the granite staircase, dragging Suresh and Ah Huat through the dark service doors of Block 37.

We didn't stop to collect our tripods, laser scanners, or diagnostic tablets. We abandoned tens of thousands of dollars worth of state equipment in those corridors.

We sprinted through the main hallway of Block 37, burst through the wooden double doors, and pelted up the sloping, covered walkway toward the upper exit of Block 24.

As we ran up the steep slope, the long, covered corridor around us transformed into an absolute nightmare.

The thick glass panes along the walkway shattered inward one by one, showering us with sharp shards. The cold, sickly scent of frangipani and rotting flesh saturated the air so heavily I vomited while running.

From the dense jungle foliage on both sides of the walkway, shadowy figures stood behind the fence line, their pale, featureless faces watching us silently through the rain-vines as we ran past.

We reached the glass doors of Block 24, burst into the main reception hall, and hurled ourselves through the front security exit onto the gravel driveway of Halton Road.

We collapsed onto the wet gravel beneath the dim yellow glow of the streetlamp, gasping for air, sobbing, our bodies covered in cold sweat, mud, and Suresh's blood from his bruised wrists.

The warm, humid Singapore night air hit our lungs like a blessing. The cicadas were singing in the surrounding trees. Traffic noise from Changi Village was faintly audible in the distance.

We looked back at Old Changi Hospital.

Block 24 sat dark, silent, and imposing atop the hill. But on the third-floor balcony of the main building, standing behind the concrete railing, illuminated by the dim moonlight, was a crowd of pale silhouettes.

They stood shoulder to shoulder, dozens of them, staring down at us on the driveway in absolute, dead silence.

Section VIII:

The Aftermath

We didn't call the police. We didn't call the Ministry of Health.

Marcus drove us directly to Singapore General Hospital in our utility van. Suresh was admitted to the emergency ward, where doctors diagnosed him with severe acute shock, a fractured collarbone, and deep soft-tissue trauma on his torso consistent with extreme physical compression.

He remained hospitalized for three weeks. When he was finally discharged, he resigned from the engineering firm immediately, sold his home in Tampines, and moved his entire family back to Chennai, India. He never spoke a single word about what happened in that basement.

Uncle Ah Huat retired from facility management the following Monday. He moved to Johor Bahru, Malaysia, and refused to answer any phone calls from our company.

Marcus Tan—the pragmatic, cold, logical engineer who believed in load calculations and concrete density—was never the same man again. He took a two-month medical leave, resigned from his senior position, and eventually took a desk job with a residential property firm in West Australia.

As for me, I submitted my floor plan sketches based on the partial data we gathered prior to midnight. I noted in the official public engineering report that the sub-basement of Block 37 suffered from "extreme structural degradation, hazardous environmental air quality, and severe risk of subterranean collapse," recommending that all lower utility entrances be permanently sealed with solid concrete poured directly into the stairwells.

In late 1997, the government filled the lower entrances of Old Changi Hospital with tons of concrete and erected tall, high-security iron fences topped with razor wire around the entire perimeter of Halton Road.

Over the years, film crews, urban explorers, and ghost hunters have tried to trespass onto the grounds of Old Changi Hospital. Many have posted stories online about ghost sightings, cold spots, and strange sounds.

They think they know the story of Old Changi Hospital. They think it's a fun local urban legend to explore on a dare during Halloween.

They don't know what lies beneath Block 37. They don't know about the operating theater behind the electrical board, or the iron drawers carved into the granite bedrock.

Old Changi Hospital is not just an abandoned building. It is a sealed grave—a dark reservoir of pain, torture, and agony trapped beneath sixty feet of concrete and soil, waiting for anyone foolish enough to break the chains.

If you ever find yourself in Changi Village, enjoying seafood at the hawker center or walking along Changi Beach at night, look up toward the dark hill on Halton Road. If you see the dark silhouette of the hospital rising above the trees... do not walk up that hill. Do not look for the open fence. And if you ever hear a voice calling for help from the dark trees near the old walkway... Turn around, get into your car, and drive away as fast as you can.

Written and submitted by: Kevin Lee

Perth, Western Australia

(Formerly Junior Structural Auditor, PWD Singapore, 1997)
