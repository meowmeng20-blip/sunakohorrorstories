OGOPOGO

Sender: Liam MacIntyre • Lake Cryptids

If you grew up anywhere near the Okanagan Valley in British Columbia, you grow up hearing the jokes. Tourists come up from Vancouver or Seattle every summer, floating on rented pontoons, drinking beer, and laughing about the "Canadian Loch Ness Monster." They call it Ogopogo. They buy little green plush toys with dopey smiles for their kids at the souvenir shops along Lakeshore Drive, treating it like a cute local mascot.

I used to laugh along with them. I thought it was just a marketing gimmick invented to boost tourism during the slow autumn months.

I don't laugh anymore. In fact, looking at the dark water of that lake now makes my chest tighten and my hands shake.

This happened last month, right as the summer tourist crowds were thinning out and the weather started turning sharp and unpredictable. My best friend, Noah, bought a beat-up twenty-foot Chris-Craft motorboat off an old-timer in Peachland. Noah is an environmental science major at UBC Okanagan, completely obsessed with aquatic ecosystems, water depth sonar mapping, and deep-lake thermal layers. Me? I’m just a guy with a drone and a camera who likes filming scenic landscapes for my YouTube channel.

On a Friday afternoon in late September, Noah called me up. The sky was overcast, bruised purple and gray, with low-hanging clouds clinging to the steep granite cliffs surrounding the lake.

The Location:

Squally Point, Okanagan Lake

"Bring your heavy gear and the underwater drone," Noah said over the phone, his voice buzzing with nervous excitement. "I’ve been tracking some weird bathymetric anomalies near Squally Point. The sonar readings down there are completely distorted. Something massive is down in the trench, moving against the thermal currents."

"Squally Point?" I groaned. "Dude, that’s the windiest, deepest part of the lake. The local Syilx First Nations legends say bad things live down there."

"Oh, come on, Liam. Are you scared of ghost stories now?" Noah laughed. "Bring the camera. We’ll be back before dark."

That arrogance—that stupid, youthful disbelief—is what almost got us killed.

We launched the boat from a quiet private dock near Kelowna around 4:00 PM. The water was unnaturally calm, flat as a sheet of dark glass, reflecting the heavy storm clouds above like a mirror. But beneath that deceptive calm, the air felt thick, heavy, and dead. There were no other boats on the water. Even the gulls and Canadian geese had completely vanished from the shoreline.

As Noah steered the boat south toward Squally Point, the temperature dropped dramatically. Within twenty minutes, my breath was pluming into white mist in the cold mountain air.

"Check the ambient temp," Noah muttered, gripping the steering wheel tightly. "The surface water just dropped ten degrees in half a mile. That’s physically impossible without a massive sub-lacustrine vent."

We cut the engine about a quarter mile off the sheer rock face of Squally Point. The cliffs rose straight up out of the dark water for hundreds of feet, scarred with ancient cracks and draped in gloomy pine trees. According to local lore, Squally Point was home to Naitaka, the lake demon, a place where travelers traditionally tossed small animals into the water to appease the spirit before crossing.

Noah powered up his portable sonar monitor, while I prepped my underwater camera drone, attaching a heavy-duty waterproof tether and checking the battery indicator.

"Drop the sonar probe," Noah whispered, his eyes glued to the green glowing screen. "Look at that... right down there at the bottom of the trench. Around four hundred feet deep."

I looked over his shoulder. On the sonar display, moving slowly across the digital grid, was a massive, undulating mass. It wasn't a school of fish or a sunken log. It was a single, massive organic shape—over forty feet long, with a series of distinct humps moving in a synchronized, rhythmic motion.

"Is that a submerged tree trunk catching an undercurrent?" I asked, though my gut was already knotting with cold dread.

"Logs don't swim upstream against a thermal gradient, Liam," Noah replied, his voice dropping to a tense whisper. "And they definitely don't have mass like that."

Underwater Drone Feed:

350 Feet Below the Surface

"Let's drop the drone," I said, my fingers trembling slightly as I powered up the mini-submersible camera equipped with a powerful LED searchlight. "Let's get a visual and get the hell out of here."

I lowered the drone over the side of the boat, watching the live video feed on my tablet screen as the tiny machine descended into the ink-black abyss. The water was remarkably murky, choked with suspended sediment and organic decay. At two hundred feet down, the light from the drone’s LEDs barely pierced five feet ahead.

At three hundred and fifty feet down, the screen flickered violently. Static hissed through the tablet's tiny speaker.

"Interference," I muttered, tapping the screen. "Must be mineral deposits in the rock face."

Then, the water on the live feed cleared up. The sediment parted as if pushed away by a massive displacement of current.

What appeared in the beam of the drone’s LED light wasn't a fish, and it certainly wasn't a log.

It was a vast, leathery expanse of skin, dark greenish-brown and mottled with pale, circular scars like ancient battle wounds. The skin was thick, textured like crocodile hide mixed with the smooth, rubbery sheen of a giant beluga whale, but shaped with long, muscular folds. As the drone hovered closer, the camera panned upward along the massive flank of the creature.

We saw a ridge of dark, spade-shaped plates running down its spine, dripping with thick, gelatinous slime.

"What the fuck is that?" Noah breathed, his face completely drained of color, his hands frozen on the boat console.

Before I could answer, the entire Chris-Craft boat shuddered violently, as if we had just slammed into a submerged boulder at full speed. The impact knocked both of us off our feet, sending our equipment crashing across the aluminum deck.

"Did we hit a rock?!" I screamed, scrambling back to my feet.

"No!" Noah yelled, pointing a shaking finger at the water beside the boat. "Look at the depth finder!"

The depth finder wasn't registering four hundred feet anymore. It was flashing red: 12 FT... 8 FT... 4 FT...

The lake bed wasn't rising. Something immense was lifting us from underneath.

The water around the boat began to churn and boil, not with white foam, but with heavy, oily swirls that smelled intensely of rotting marsh plants, sulfur, and ancient, stagnant mud—a smell so foul it instantly made my stomach heave.

The Encounter:

Naitaka Emerges

Suddenly, right beside the starboard side of the hull, the dark water broke.

It didn't splash; it parted silently, rising like a mountain of black muscle. A massive, equine head emerged from the depths, easily the size of a full-grown horse, but entirely alien. Its skin was slick and wet, glistening under the gloomy grey light. It had no external ears, just two sunken, obsidian-black eyes that looked ancient, intelligent, and filled with a cold, terrifying predatory awareness.

Two long, fleshy horns or appendages swept back from the crown of its head, trailing dripping strands of dark lake weed.

The creature didn't roar. It didn't make a sound. But as it opened its massive jaws—revealing rows of flat, crushing teeth stained with dark silt—a low, infrasonic vibration rolled across the water. It wasn't just a sound you heard with your ears; it was a physical force that rattled my teeth in my gums, vibrated in my chest cavity, and made my vision blur with sudden, blinding vertigo.

My brain felt like it was frying. Panic, absolute and paralyzing, locked my muscles in place.

The creature turned its massive head, fixing its dead, black eyes directly on me. For one horrifying second, I felt as though it wasn't just looking at my body—it was reading my thoughts, weighing my life, deciding whether to drag our entire boat down into the four-hundred-foot trench below.

Without thinking, Noah slammed his foot down on the throttle.

The Chris-Craft’s engine roared to life, screaming at maximum RPMs. The boat surged forward with a violent lunge, tearing away from the spot just as the creature sank back into the water. There was no massive splash—just a slow, terrifying subsidence, as if a mountain had simply dissolved back into the dark depths.

Noah didn't look back. He gripped the steering wheel with white-knuckled hands, cutting a sharp wake straight north toward the Kelowna marina, pushing the boat harder than its aging engine was ever meant to run.

We didn't slow down until we crashed into the wooden slips at the dock, tumbling out onto the solid ground of the parking lot, coughing, gasping for air, and shaking so violently we could barely stand.

When I checked my equipment bag back in the safety of my truck, my hands were still trembling. The underwater drone was gone—the heavy-duty tether had been sliced completely clean, as if sheared by a razor-sharp steel blade.

When I opened my camera tablet to check if any backup cache had saved to the cloud, the file was there. A single three-second video clip.

It showed the massive, obsidian-black eye of the creature staring directly into the camera lens from inches away, surrounded by pitch-black water.

I didn't upload it to my YouTube channel. I didn't share it with anyone. That very night, I deleted the file, smashed the micro-SD card with a hammer, and threw the pieces into a highway dumpster miles away from home.

If you ever find yourself visiting the Okanagan Valley on a warm summer afternoon, go ahead and rent that pontoon boat. Buy the little green plush monster for your kids. Drink your beer in the sun and laugh at the old ghost stories. Just make sure you stay close to the shore. And if you ever find yourself drifting down near Squally Point when the water turns dead calm and the air smells like sulfur... pray to whatever god you believe in that it stays asleep down in the dark.
