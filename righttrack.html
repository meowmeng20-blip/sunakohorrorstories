THE MAN ON THE RIGHT TRACK

Sender: A.R Cruz • Japanese Legends

The summer in the small town of Inunaki, Fukuoka, always brought two things: the deafening drone of cicadas and a suffocating heat radiating from the surrounding mountains.

I was sixteen years old the last time I stayed there. With my parents tied down by their jobs in Tokyo, they sent me away for a two-week summer break at my grandparents’ traditional home. Their house was a classic Japanese minka—complete with sliding paper shoji doors, high wooden ceilings, and a sprawling backyard bordered by a dense, unkept bamboo forest.

On my very first evening, as we sat down for dinner over cold soba noodles and tempura, I noticed a strange weight in my grandfather’s expression. He reached into his pocket and handed me a small, woven golden Omamori (protective charm) from the local Shinto shrine.

The Warning:

The Abandoned Railway Track

“Kenji,” Grandpa began, his voice dry and crackling like autumn leaves. “Things have changed in this valley over the years, but some things never leave. Do you see the abandoned railway track running along the edge of the eastern mountain?”

Confused, I nodded. “Yes, Grandpa. The old line that used to lead to the abandoned coal mine?”

“Between seven o’clock in the evening and sunrise,” he continued, locking his gaze dead into mine, “you must never walk along that track. And if you ever find yourself near it and hear a rustling sound in the tall grass on the right side of the rails... do not turn around. Do not look, do not speak, and above all, do not stop walking.”

I let out a weak chuckle. “Grandpa, it’s 2026. You don't honestly still believe in mountain spirits, do you?”

Grandpa didn't smile. Instead, his grip tightened around my wrist with startling force. “That is no spirit, Kenji. It is older than this entire town. The locals call it Katakata-sama. It has no face and no legs, but it crawls faster than a sprinting deer. If you look into its eyes, your soul stays trapped on those tracks forever.”

Grandma remained completely silent beside him, her breathing heavy as she wiped down the dining table. A cold shiver ran down my spine, so I simply nodded to put the matter to rest.

Five days passed without incident. I sank into the easy rhythm of summer vacation—fishing in the local river, riding my bicycle down quiet paved lanes, and reading manga under the breeze of an electric fan. Grandpa’s warning faded completely from my mind.

Until the sixth night.

I had walked down to the town’s only convenience store to pick up snacks and iced tea. Mesmerized by the quiet night and chatting with the cashier, I lost track of time. When I finally stepped out of the store, darkness had completely consumed the valley. The digital clock on my phone read 07:45 PM.

Eager to get home before my grandfather scolded me, I decided to take a shortcut: the narrow dirt path alongside the abandoned railway line.

The Encounter:

Katakata-sama

The atmosphere beside the tracks was pitch black, illuminated only by the pale moonlight and the screen of my phone. The evening air felt unusually chilly, and the ambient noise of the cicadas abruptly died down. A suffocating silence settled over the path.

That was when I heard it.

Kata... kata... kata... kata...

I froze in my tracks. The sound was coming from the right side of the railway—hidden deep inside the tall weeds and thick bamboo. It sounded like dry wood violently striking concrete, or brittle bones clacking together in rapid succession.

Kata... kata... kata... kata...

My heart slammed against my ribs. It’s just a stray cat or a wild boar, I tried to convince myself. I kept my head pointed strictly forward, remembering my grandfather’s warning.

I forced my legs to move faster, but as my pace quickened, the clacking sound on my right kept speed with me.

KATA-KATA-KATA-KATA!

Out of the corner of my right eye, something caught the moonlight that made my blood run ice-cold. Emerging from the tall grass was a long, emaciated arm—pale ash-gray skin with long, jagged black fingernails—gripping the rusty steel rail. The fingers were bent completely backward!

And then, a voice echoed. It didn't sound human. It sounded like a chorus of a thousand distant whispers speaking in unison from the bottom of a deep well:

“Look at me... Kenji... Don't you recognize me?... Look at me...”

Terror took over. I let out a bloodcurdling scream and sprinted forward!

KATA-KATA-KATA-KATA-KATA!

The noise to my right exploded into a furious frenzy—whatever was chasing me was crawling at an impossible speed! I felt a sudden gust of freezing air strike the back of my neck, accompanied by the foul stench of rotting soil and stale blood.

“Grandpa! Grandma!” I screamed as I ran blindly into the dark. My foot caught on a jagged rock, and I crashed violently onto the sharp gravel. My phone flew from my hand and shattered in the darkness.

When I raised my head, I saw the end of the tracks and the wooden gate of my grandparents' house just twenty meters away! But before I could push myself up, an impossibly cold hand clamped onto my right ankle.

“Got you...” the raspy voice whispered directly against my ear.

I squeezed my eyes shut and grabbed the Omamori charm resting against my chest. “Help me! Grandpa!” I shrieked, kicking wildly with my left leg.

Suddenly, the front door of the house burst open! Grandpa rushed out holding a lit paper lantern and a heavy brass bell, while Grandma followed close behind, hurling handfuls of coarse white salt into the air.

CLANG! CLANG! CLANG!

As Grandpa rang the brass bell and shouted Shinto prayers into the night, a horrific, ear-splitting shriek tore through the air—a sound like metal scraping against glass. The freezing grip on my ankle vanished.

CRAWL! I felt the heavy drag of something scrambling backward at incredible speed into the pitch-black bamboo forest.

Grandpa hoisted me onto my feet and dragged me inside, slamming the doors shut and immediately pasting Ofuda (holy paper charms) across every doorframe and window.

I didn't sleep a single second that night. We sat on the tatami mats in the center of the living room, encircled by four small bowls of salt. By morning, the salt in the bowl facing the eastern tracks had turned completely jet-black.

At six o'clock sharp, Grandpa escorted me straight into a waiting taxi. I wasn't even allowed to go back to my room to pack my clothes.

“You are going back to Tokyo right now,” Grandpa said, his hands trembling as he pushed me into the backseat. “And you will never return to Inunaki. It saw your face, Kenji. It caught your scent. As long as you stay far away in the city, it cannot reach you.”

Tears streamed down my face as I waved goodbye to them from the taxi window on my way to the bullet train station. That was the last time I ever saw my grandfather. He passed away two years later, but in his final written will, he strictly forbade me from returning to the village to attend his funeral.

Five years have passed since that night. I now live in a modern, high-rise apartment complex in the heart of bustling Tokyo. I am safe. There are no railway tracks near my building, and I am hundreds of miles away from the mountains of Fukuoka.

But last night, as I lay in bed, I woke up to a faint, horrifying sound.

It wasn't coming from outside my window... but from inside my bedroom.

From the dark corner on the right side of my bed, right beneath my wardrobe, I heard that dry, clacking rhythm echoing in the quiet room:

“Kata... kata... kata... kata...”

Then, my phone buzzed on the nightstand. A text message arrived from my grandfather's old number—a number that had been disconnected three years ago:

“Forgive me, Kenji... A wildfire burned down the old tracks in town. It has nowhere left to stay... so it went looking for you.”
