MONTE CRISTO HOMESTEAD

Sender: Rizky Pratama • Haunted Estates

My name is Rizky Pratama. I am thirty-nine years old, born and raised in the bustling metropolis of Jakarta, Indonesia. For over fourteen years, I have worked as a senior commercial lighting designer and architectural illumination specialist. My career involves designing intricate lighting setups for luxury hotels, heritage restorations, and high-end museums across Southeast Asia and Australia.

I am a man driven by lumens, circuit loads, color temperatures, voltage stability, and architectural blueprints. I do not believe in ghosts. I do not believe in curses or haunted houses. In my field, when lights flicker, dim unexpectedly, or cast unsettling shadows, it is always caused by faulty wiring, voltage drops, poor grounding, or degraded filaments in vintage fixtures.

That was my absolute professional conviction until September of 2025, when my firm was hired to design a specialized, low-impact LED museum lighting system for the historic Monte Cristo Homestead in Junee, New South Wales.

The Estate History:

Australia's Most Haunted House

For those unfamiliar with Australian history, the Monte Cristo Homestead sits on a lonely, elevated ridge overlooking the small town of Junee. It is widely documented and feared throughout the southern hemisphere as "Australia’s Most Haunted House."

Built in 1885 by a wealthy local pioneer named Christopher William Crawley, the two-story Victorian manor is a grand monument of ornate ironwork, dark mahogany staircases, stained-glass windows, and sprawling wide verandas. But beneath its elegant grandeur lies a dark history filled with tragedy, lingering madness, and sudden death.

During the Crawley family’s long residency, the mansion saw an appalling sequence of tragedies:

  • A young baby girl was allegedly dropped down the grand mahogany staircase by a maid who claimed an unseen force pushed her arms. The infant died instantly on the hallway tiles.
  • A pregnant maid plunged to her death from the second-story balcony, landing on the courtyard paving stones below—a death officially ruled a suicide, though rumor whispered of an unwanted pregnancy tied to the master of the house.
  • A young stable boy was burned alive in his sleep inside the carriage house after his straw mattress inexplicably caught fire.
  • Following the death of Christopher Crawley in 1910, his grief-stricken widow, Mrs. Virginia Crawley, spent the remaining twenty-three years of her life locked inside the upstairs chapel room, leaving it only twice, dressed in perpetual black mourning attire, and reading her Bible until her death in 1933.

When the last Crawley family member died, the house sat abandoned for decades, vandalized and left to rot, until it was purchased in 1963 by Reginald and Olive Ryan, who lovingly restored it to its former Victorian splendor. But the ghosts never left.

My contract had nothing to do with paranormal investigation. The local tourism board was planning a series of late-night historical heritage tours, and they needed an architectural lighting system that could subtly highlight the Victorian antiques without damaging delicate oil paintings or consuming high power from the estate's old electrical grid.

Our team was small, consisting of three people:

  • Myself, Rizky Pratama, handling lighting design, fixture installation, and power grid auditing.
  • Hannah Clarke, a twenty-seven-year-old electrician from Melbourne who handled cable runs and digital dimmer controls.
  • Mr. Thomas Thorne, our night caretaker and grounds supervisor, a taciturn local historian who carried the heavy brass ring of Victorian keys.

We arrived at the estate on a cool, overcast afternoon. Standing at the wrought-iron front gates, Monte Cristo Homestead looms over the landscape like a silent spectator. The house exudes an undeniable, heavy tension—its dark glass windows staring down at the surrounding gardens like hollow eyes.

"I’ll give you the golden rule right now, Rizky," Mr. Thorne said as he inserted a heavy brass key into the massive front door lock. "We stay on the ground floor as much as possible after sunset. You don't go into the upstairs chapel room alone after 10:00 PM. And if you feel a cold hand touch the back of your neck... you stop what you're doing, walk out to the driveway, and wait for daylight."

Hannah laughed lightly, unhooking her tool belt. "Cold drafts are pretty standard in 19th-century timber homes, Mr. Thorne."

Thorne didn't smile. He looked at her with cold, tired eyes. "Drafts don't leave finger-shaped bruises on your skin, Miss Clarke."

We began our work at 5:00 PM.

As night fell over the Junee countryside, a deep, suffocating darkness enveloped the estate. The crickets outside grew silent, replaced only by the low whistling of the cold night wind passing through the wide veranda railings.

By 9:00 PM, Hannah and I had completed installing subtle floor-strip lighting in the formal dining room, the drawing room, and the ground-floor hallway. The data on our diagnostic monitors was clean, though the estate's electrical system was surprisingly prone to sudden, minor voltage fluctuations.

The Upper Landing:

Mrs. Crawley's Chapel Room

At 10:15 PM, we moved our equipment up the grand mahogany staircase to illuminate the upper floor—the floor containing the master bedrooms, the nursery, and Mrs. Crawley’s private chapel room.

The moment my boots stepped onto the upper landing, the temperature plummeted. My digital environmental gauge dropped from twenty degrees Celsius down to four degrees in less than ten seconds. My breath formed thick clouds of white condensation in the narrow corridor.

"Check the voltage on the main junction box, Hannah," I said, shining my flashlight down the dark hall lined with antique wallpaper and framed black-and-white portraits of the Crawley family. "The circuit is dragging."

"Junction box is stable," Hannah replied, her voice trembling slightly from the sudden cold. "Rizky... look at the portraits."

I turned my tactical spotlight onto the dark, gilt-framed oil painting of Mrs. Virginia Crawley hanging near the chapel door.

In the painting, Mrs. Crawley sat stoically in a black lace gown, holding a leather-bound Bible. But as I stared at the canvas, the paint seemed to distort under my light beam. The sharp corners of her painted mouth tightened into a deep, furious sneer, and her eyes—painted with dark brown oils—appeared to follow my movement with unmistakable hatred.

"It's just glare from the high-lumen spotlight," I muttered, trying to convince myself as much as my junior tech. "Let's set up the LED drivers outside the chapel door and finish up."

We set to work. I sat on an vintage leather trunk in the hallway, adjusting the wireless dimmer frequencies on my laptop.

At 11:10 PM, every single LED fixture we had installed across the entire second floor flickered wildly, emitting a sharp, metallic hum before turning a deep, unnatural crimson shade.

"Hannah, did you alter the color spectrum on channel four?" I asked, tapping my touchpad.

"I didn't touch the console, Rizky!" Hannah gasped from inside the master bedroom. "My diagnostic tablet just completely froze!"

Thud... thud... thud...

The sound echoed from the top of the grand mahogany staircase behind us.

It was the heavy, rhythmic thud of a heavy object sliding down the wooden steps, followed by a high-pitched, infant cry that echoed through the empty hallway—a thin, reedy, terrifying shriek that sounded as though it were trapped behind the plaster walls.

Hannah ran out of the master bedroom, her face drained of all color. "Rizky, did you hear that?!"

"Stay behind me," I snapped, grabbing a heavy brass lighting bar from my kit.

We walked slowly toward the staircase landing. The hallway was dead silent now, save for the hum of the corrupted red lights.

Lying at the top step of the staircase was an object that had not been there five minutes ago: a small, tarnished Victorian brass rattle, slowly rocking back and forth on the bare floorboards as if it had just been dropped.

Click...

Behind us, the heavy oak door of Mrs. Crawley’s chapel room swung open on its iron hinges with a long, agonizing creak.

A blast of air hit us from inside the room—an air so impossibly cold it stung our faces like tiny ice needles. But worse than the cold was the smell. The refined smell of old polish and dry timber vanished instantly, replaced by an overpowering, sickening stench of decayed flowers, wet wool, and old blood.

The Manifestation:

The Veiled Widow

"Rizky..." Hannah whispered, her voice choking as she pointed a trembling finger into the dark chapel room. "Look at the chair."

Inside the small, dark chapel room, illuminated only by the faint red glow of our flickering equipment lights, sat a figure in the high-backed velvet armchair facing the altar.

It was a woman. She was dressed in a thick, heavy Victorian mourning gown made of pitch-black silk that absorbed the light around her. Her face was hidden beneath a long black lace veil, but her hands—resting on a heavy, leather-bound Bible in her lap—were visible.

They were not the hands of a living human. The skin was yellowed, paper-thin, and stretched so tight over gnarled bone that her knuckles were exposed. Her long, black-stained fingernails dug deep into the leather cover of the Bible, tearing the binding with a dry, scratching sound.

Scritch... scritch... scritch...

"You bring false light into my house..." a voice rasped from beneath the black veil.

It was not a loud voice, but it vibrated directly inside my eardrums—a dry, ancient, wheezing sound like dry leaves scraping across a stone floor.

"No light belongs here... save for the light of God..."

"We... we are leaving now," I stammered, grabbing Hannah’s arm and stepping backward toward the stairs. "We are leaving."

The figure in the chair did not stand up. Instead, her head snapped sideways at a sickening, ninety-degree angle.

Through the translucent black veil, two milky-white, dead eyes opened, glowing with an pale, unnatural luminescence.

The moment her eyes met mine, the laptop screen in my hand exploded into jagged shards of glass. The digital dimmer pack mounted on the wall short-circuited with a blinding blue flash and a shower of sparks, plunging the hallway into absolute, pitch-black darkness.

"RIZKY!" Hannah screamed hysterically as an unseen force grabbed her by her jacket, pulling her violently backward toward the dark chapel room.

I lunged forward in the dark, grabbing Hannah by both wrists. I pulled with all my strength, my feet slipping on the polished floorboards. It felt as though I was battling an industrial winch—the force pulling on Hannah was cold, absolute, and inhuman.

"LEAVE THIS HOUSE!" a chorus of voices shrieked through the darkness...

Not just the old woman's raspy tone, but the agonizing wails of a screaming infant, the horrific gargling cry of a falling woman, and the agonizing screams of a boy burning in straw.

The sound swelled into a deafening roar that shook the very floorboards beneath our feet. Plaster dust rained down from the ceiling, and the glass globes of the antique wall sconces shattered one by one in rapid succession down the corridor.

With a final, desperate heave, I yanked Hannah free from the unseen grip. We tumbled backward down the grand mahogany staircase, crashing onto the hard tile floor of the ground entrance hall.

"OUT! GET OUT!" Mr. Thorne roared from the front entrance, slamming open the heavy front door and shining an industrial halogen searchlight up the staircase.

In the beam of Thorne's halogen searchlight, I looked back up toward the top landing.

Standing at the crest of the mahogany stairs was not just one figure.

Standing at the top of the steps was the tall, veiled silhouette of Mrs. Virginia Crawley. Standing beside her was the shadowy, charred form of a young boy whose limbs twitched erratically, and behind them, suspended upside down in mid-air above the stairwell, was the pale, translucent figure of a young woman in a maid's uniform, her face contorted in an eternal scream.

"RUN!" Thorne bellowed, grabbing us by our collars and hauling us through the open front doors out into the night air.

We sprinted across the gravel driveway, diving into our service van just as Thorne slammed the heavy iron gates of the estate shut behind us.

Through the van's side window, I looked back at the second story of Monte Cristo Homestead.

Every single window on the upper floor was glowing with an intense, fiery orange light—as if the entire upper story were engulfed in a massive blaze. But there was no smoke. There were no sirens. Just the silent, blinding, unnatural light burning behind the dark glass panes, and standing at the central balcony window was the single, black-veiled figure of Mrs. Crawley, watching us drive away into the night.

We drove back to Sydney that same night without stopping.

Hannah resigned from my firm forty-eight hours later. She moved back to Melbourne, sold all her electrical tools, and completely abandoned her career in lighting engineering. She suffers from severe, night terrors and cannot sleep in any room without total, bright illumination.

I submitted a brief, formal letter to the Junee heritage committee, terminating our contract immediately due to severe electrical grid instability, structural hazards, and unsafe working conditions. I paid for all destroyed LED equipment out of my own pocket just to sever all ties with the project.

It has been over a year since that night in New South Wales. I am back in Jakarta now, strictly taking commercial jobs—modern shopping malls, high-rise office towers, and brand-new airport terminals. I refuse any contract involving historical buildings, Victorian architecture, or heritage restorations.

I know how crazy this story sounds to anyone who works in modern engineering. People love to visit Monte Cristo Homestead during weekend ghost tours, laughing as they take photos of old furniture and rusty carriages, treating the estate like a fun tourist attraction. I know what lives inside those grand Victorian halls. Monte Cristo is not just a museum of history. It is a living tomb of grief, suffering, and dark energy that feeds on anyone who dares to bring light into its darkness. And if you ever visit Junee and take a tour of that grand house on the hill... if you walk up those mahogany stairs and feel the air suddenly turn to ice... Do not look at the paintings. Do not go into the chapel. Just turn around and run before the house closes its doors on you forever.
