THE MIDNIGHT PASSENGER: A GHOST ON ROUTE 9

Sender: Randy • Roadside Hauntings

Chapter 1:

The Foggy Highway

The clock on the dashboard read 1:17 AM.

Rain pelted relentlessly against the windshield of my car, the wipers struggling to keep up with the downpour. Route 9 was infamous for two things: being a shortcut through the isolated pine forests, and being the deadliest stretch of road in the county. Thick, ghostly fog clung to the asphalt, reducing my visibility to barely ten feet in front of my headlights.

My arms were stiff, gripping the steering wheel so hard my knuckles turned white. I was exhausted after driving five hours straight from a late shift in the city. The only sounds keeping me awake were the low hum of the engine and the rhythmic thump-thump, thump-thump of my tires over the road cracks.

Suddenly, my headlights caught a flicker of motion on the shoulder of the road.

I slammed my foot on the brake, my tires screeching softly against the wet pavement. My heart leaped into my throat as the car slid to a halt just a few yards ahead of a figure standing alone in the storm.

It was a girl.

She wasn't wearing a raincoat, nor was she holding an umbrella. She wore a simple, light-blue vintage dress that was completely soaked, clinging to her fragile frame. Her head was bowed, dark wet hair plastered across her face.

I rolled down the passenger side window just an inch, cold wind and rain rushing into the warm car. "Hey! Are you okay? It's freezing out there!"

Slowly, she raised her head. In the dim amber glow of my headlights, her face was pale—almost translucent—with soft, hollow eyes that looked utterly exhausted.

"My... my car broke down a few miles back," her voice was faint, barely carrying over the sound of the rain. "Can you give me a ride home? I live just off the highway, past the old iron bridge."

Normally, I would never pick up a stranger at one in the morning. But looking at how shivering and helpless she seemed, leaving her out in the middle of nowhere felt like a death sentence.

"Get in," I unlocked the passenger door. "I'll turn the heater on."

Chapter 2:

The Silent Car

She pulled the door open and slid into the leather seat. A sudden wave of freezing air swept into the car—not just the chill of the rain, but a heavy, unnatural cold that seemed to settle deep in my bones.

"I'm Marcus," I said, putting the car back in drive and stepping on the gas.

"Clara," she replied softly.

As I drove, I tried to make polite conversation to break the uncomfortable silence. "What were you doing out here so late, Clara? Route 9 is dangerous at night."

She didn't answer right away. She kept her eyes fixed out the passenger window, staring into the dark abyss of the pine trees. "I was trying to get home," she murmured. "I’ve been trying to get home for a very long time."

I glanced at her briefly. Her hands were folded neatly in her lap. Strangely, despite her dress being soaked when she got in, there was no puddle on the floorboard, no dripping sound. The air inside the car felt increasingly dense, making it strangely hard to catch my breath.

"It's getting really cold in here," I remarked, adjusting the heater knob to maximum. But no matter how hard the vents blew hot air, the temperature inside the vehicle kept plummeting.

I reached into the backseat and grabbed my thick flannel jacket. "Here, take this. You're shaking."

Without turning her head, she accepted the jacket and wrapped it around her shoulders. "Thank you, Marcus. You're very kind... unlike the others who drove past me."

A strange shiver ran down my spine. Something felt off, but I brushed it aside, attributing it to fatigue and the creepy atmosphere of the storm.

Chapter 3:

The Vanishing

About ten minutes later, we approached a rusty, flickering street sign marking the turnoff past the old iron bridge.

"It's the white house at the end of this dirt lane," Clara whispered, pointing toward a narrow road shrouded in darkness.

I turned the steering wheel, my tires crunching over gravel. At the end of the lane stood a quaint, two-story house. The porch light was left on, casting a warm yellow square onto the overgrown front lawn.

"Here we are," I said, putting the car into park. "Safe and sound."

Silence.

"Clara?"

I turned my head to look at the passenger seat.

My breath caught in my throat. My blood turned to ice.

The passenger seat was empty.

The seatbelt was still buckled. The door was securely closed. But Clara wasn't there.

"What the... Clara?!" I yelled, scrambling to unbuckle my seatbelt. I flung open the car door and checked underneath, around the vehicle, and back down the dark lane. There were no footprints in the mud, no sound of running feet, no rustling bushes. She had completely vanished into thin air.

Panicking, my eyes fell back onto the empty passenger seat.

Draped neatly over the headrest was my flannel jacket. It was bone-dry.

Chapter 4:

The Truth at the Doorstep

Trembling violently, my heart hammering against my ribs, I marched up the wooden steps of the porch. I needed answers. I was convinced I was losing my mind, or that this was some sick, elaborate prank.

I knocked heavily on the front door. Thump. Thump. Thump.

After a few excruciating seconds, a porch light clicked brighter, and the door creaked open. Standing in the doorway was an elderly woman with silver hair, wrapped in a faded shawl. Her eyes were tired and marked with deep lines of grief.

"Can I help you, young man?" she asked quietly, eyeing my frantic state.

"I... I'm sorry to bother you so late, ma'am," I stammered, my voice shaking. "I just picked up a girl on Route 9. She said her name was Clara and that she lived here. She was in my car a minute ago, but then she... she disappeared. I don't know what's happening."

The moment I spoke the name Clara, the old woman’s posture softened, and a tear welled up in her faded blue eyes. She didn't look surprised. She looked heartbroken.

"You picked her up near the bend before the iron bridge, didn't you?" she asked softly.

"Y-yes... how did you know?"

The woman stepped aside and invited me into the entryway. On a small wooden table rested a framed black-and-white photograph surrounded by dried flowers.

I walked closer, and my stomach dropped into a bottomless pit.

In the picture was the same girl—with the same hollow eyes, the same soft smile, and the exact same light-blue vintage dress.

"That's Clara," the old woman whispered, her voice choking up. "My daughter. Exactly twelve years ago tonight, she was driving home from her college graduation in a terrible rainstorm. Her car skidded off Route 9 near the bridge. She died before the paramedics could reach her."

I couldn't speak. The room felt like it was spinning.

"Every year, on the anniversary of her accident," the mother continued, looking out into the foggy night, "some kind driver knocks on my door, telling me the same story. She's just trying to find her way home, son. She just wants to come home."

Chapter 5:

The Rearview Mirror

I don't remember how I walked back to my car, or how I managed to put key to ignition. My mind was completely numb, trapped in a nightmare of reality.

I backed out of the driveway, my hands trembling uncontrollably on the steering wheel. I sped down the dirt lane, eager to leave that house, that road, and that memory behind forever.

As I turned back onto the main highway, the rain began to clear, leaving only the thick, suffocating fog. I let out a long, shaky exhale, trying to ground myself.

It's over, I told myself. Just keep driving. Don't look back.

Gasping for air, I adjusted my position in the driver's seat and instinctively glanced up at the rearview mirror to check the road behind me.

In the faint, red glow of my taillights... sitting silently in the middle of the backseat... was Clara.

Her pale face was inches from my headrest.

She smiled softly, her eyes staring directly into mine through the glass.

"Thank you for the ride, Marcus..." a freezing whisper echoed right beside my ear. "Now... take me back."
