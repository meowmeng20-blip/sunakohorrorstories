POVEGLIA ISLAND

Sender: Rizky Ramadhan • Forbidden Exploration

My name is Rizky. I’m thirty-one years old, born and raised in South Jakarta. If you had told me two years ago that I’d be sitting in my room in Indonesia, writing down a confession about a crime I committed in the Venetian Lagoon, I would have laughed in your face.

I’m an urban explorer and a freelance video editor. For years, my channel grew by trespassing into abandoned mental hospitals, ruined colonial sugar factories in Java, and decayed resort hotels in Bali. I thought I knew horror. I thought I had seen every trick a dark room and an overactive imagination could play on a human mind.

I was wrong. What exists on Poveglia Island isn't an echo of the past. It is an active, hungry entity that feeds on human minds.

It happened in November of 2025. I had saved up enough money to take a three-week trip through Southern Europe with my long-time filming partner, Bayu, and an Italian-American audio engineer we hired in Venice named Marco. The goal was to film an undercover three-part documentary series on off-limits, illegal historic sites across Italy.

Poveglia was our crown jewel.

The History:

Venetian Plague Pits & The Asylum

For those who don’t know, Poveglia is a small, crescent-shaped island located in the Venetian Lagoon between Venice and Lido. In the 14th century, during the height of the Black Death, the Venetian Republic turned the island into a giant plague pit (lazaretto). Tens of thousands of dying men, women, and children were shipped there to be quarantined. When they died, their bodies were dumped into mass burial trenches and set on fire. Local historians estimate that over 160,000 people died on that tiny patch of land. It is said that over fifty percent of the island's soil is composed of human ash and decayed bone.

If that wasn't enough, in 1922, the Italian government built a massive psychiatric hospital on the island. The director of the asylum was rumored to be a sadistic doctor who performed lobotomies and barbaric neurological experiments on his patients using hand drills, chisels, and hammers. The legend says the doctor was eventually driven mad by the screaming ghosts of plague victims, climbed the asylum’s central bell tower, and jumped to his death.

Today, the island is completely off-limits to tourists. The Venetian water police (Polizia Laguna) strictly patrol the waters, and local fishermen refuse to go anywhere near it, believing that human bones get tangled in their fishing nets.

To get there, Bayu and I had to bribe a local water-taxi driver named Paolo in a dark alley near Murano. It took eighteen hundred Euros in cash to convince him to risk his boat license.

"I drop you off at the south canal inlet at 11:30 PM," Paolo told us in broken, harsh English, his eyes darting nervously around the quay. "I turn off my lights. I give you three hours. At 2:30 AM, I return ONE time. If you are not on that crumbling brick pier, I leave. I will not wait a single minute extra. If the water police catch me, I am ruined. If the island catches you... God help your souls."

At 11:15 PM, the night was pitch black and freezing cold. A thick, impenetrable Venetian fog—what the locals call nebbia—rolled over the lagoon, turning the distant lights of Lido into faint, orange smudges. The motor of Paolo’s wooden boat hummed low as we glided through the dead water.

When the dark silhouette of Poveglia emerged from the fog, my stomach dropped.

The island looked like a rotting black tooth rising out of the sea. The overgrown canopy of dead willow and oak trees drooped over crumbling brick walls. The bell tower of the old hospital stood tall and jagged against the misty night sky, looking like a executioner's gallows.

Paolo killed the motor twenty yards out and coasted silently up to a half-submerged wooden dock.

"Go," Paolo whispered, his face pale under his woolen cap. "Do not shine your flashlights toward the open water. Keep your radio volume low. And do not touch the dirt in the middle of the island."

Bayu, Marco, and I hauled our heavy gear bags—containing two night-vision 4K cameras, directional microphones, a thermal imaging scanner, and extra battery packs—onto the slippery wooden planks. As soon as my boots hit the dock, Paolo slammed his boat into reverse and vanished into the fog. The sound of his motor faded in seconds, leaving us in absolute, suffocating silence.

The air smelled foul—a nauseating mixture of stagnant saltwater, damp moss, burning fuel, and something sickly sweet, like rotting fruit mixed with copper.

"Check the comms," Bayu whispered, adjusting his rig. Bayu was usually the braver one between us, but his voice sounded thin, stretched tight with tension.

"Mic 1 active, Mic 2 active," Marco replied, staring down at his digital audio recorder. "That's weird... I'm picking up a massive amount of low-frequency static on the omnidirectional mic. It sounds like electromagnetic hum, but there are no power lines within miles of this place."

"Let's move toward the main asylum building before the fog gets any thicker," I said, clicking on my tactical flashlight, casting a narrow beam through the dense mist.

We cut our way through thickets of blackberry brambles and twisted ivy that had reclaimed the brick walkways. The ground beneath our boots felt unnaturally soft, spongy, and powdery. I remembered Paolo's warning about the soil being half human ash, and a wave of nausea washed over me.

Inside the Wards:

The Haunting of the Lobotomy Wards

We reached the arched entrance of the main psychiatric hospital. The massive wooden doors had long since rotted away, leaving a gaping, black maw. Brick walls were covered in peeling plaster, rusty iron pipes hung from collapsing ceilings like disemboweled intestines, and shattered glass crunched under our feet.

We set up our main camera hub in what used to be the hospital’s central administration lobby.

"Setting up thermal camera," Bayu announced, pointing the lens down a long, dark corridor lined with ruined patient cells. "Ambient temperature is two degrees Celsius."

For the first thirty minutes, everything was quiet. We filmed B-roll of the decaying medical wards, old rusted iron bedframes, and rusted bathtubs left in the middle of hallways. It felt like every other abandoned asylum we had explored in Europe. I started to relax, thinking the legends were just hype created to scare off trespassers.

Then, at 12:15 AM, the temperature plummeted.

It didn't drop slowly. In a single second, the air temperature inside the hallway crashed from two degrees Celsius to minus eight. Our breath erupted into thick, white plumes. Frost instantly began to form on the lenses of our cameras, spider-webbing across the glass.

"What the hell?" Marco gasped, shivering violently as he adjusted his headphones. "Guys... listen to channel two. Put your earpieces in right now."

Bayu and I pulled our wireless monitors over our ears.

Through the digital static of the directional microphone, a sound was coming from the floor directly above us—the third floor, where the old lobotomy wards were located.

It was the distinct, rhythmic sound of heavy iron bedframes being dragged across concrete floors. SCREEECH... SCREEECH... SCREEECH...

Dozens of them, moving all at once, as if an entire ward of panicked patients were shoving their beds against the doors.

"Is... is someone up there?" Bayu whispered, his eyes wide with terror, his hand trembling as he held the camera steady. "Did the water police track us?"

"That's not police," Marco muttered, his face completely drained of color as he stared at the digital audio meters peaking into the red. "Listen closely under the scraping."

Beneath the deafening screech of dragging iron, there was a chorus of voices. Hundreds of them. Whispering, sobbing, gasping for air, choking on fluid, and chanting in a frantic, terrifying rhythm. It sounded like an archaic, guttural Latin, thousands of voices layered on top of each other until it sounded like a roaring hive of angry insects.

"We need to get the thermal camera pointed at the stairs," I said, my heart hammering against my ribs so hard it felt like a trapped bird. "Bayu, pan left!"

Bayu swung the thermal camera toward the central spiral staircase leading to the upper floors.

On the small LCD monitor, the cold blue room suddenly filled with heat. But it wasn't human heat.

A massive, writhing column of deep, burning red and white silhouettes—dozens of misshapen human forms, grotesquely elongated, with limbs twisting at unnatural angles—were pouring down the staircase in a silent, cascading waterfall of light. They didn't have faces on the thermal screen; they were solid masses of intense, boiling energy.

"THEY'RE COMING DOWN!" Bayu screamed, dropping his tripod.

The flashlight beam in my hand flickered violently and died. The backup headlamps on our vests went dark at the exact same moment. We were instantly plunged into pitch-black darkness.

Panic erupted. "Marco! Bayu! Move back to the entrance!" I yelled, reaching out blindly in the dark.

My hand slammed into a cold, wet wall. The smell in the room shifted instantly—the damp mold was gone, replaced by the overpowering, sickening stench of burning hair, scorched flesh, and sweet, rotting blood. The air grew so hot it felt like standing in front of an open blast furnace.

The Encounter:

The Doctor's Whisper

Suddenly, a cold, dry hand clamped down onto my left shoulder.

The fingers were impossibly long and thin, pressing into my collarbone with enough force to bruise the muscle. I froze, my blood turning to ice.

Right next to my left ear, a voice whispered. It didn't sound like it came through the air; it felt like it was generated directly inside my brain, vibrating against the inside of my skull.

"Non c'è cura qui," the voice rasped. A man's voice—smooth, cultured, yet dripping with absolute, monstrous malice. "There is no cure here. Stand still for the drill."

A high-pitched, metallic ringing sound filled my ears—the sound of a hand-cranked surgical drill spinning against bone.

"RIZKY! GET UP!"

A bright white light slammed into my eyes. Bayu had managed to strike an emergency magnesium flare he kept in his tactical vest. The brilliant, sputtering red-and-white chemical fire illuminated the room in stark, terrifying shadows.

The entity holding my shoulder vanished the second the flare ignited, but the physical pressure lingered, leaving three deep, dark bruises on my skin that would take months to heal.

"WHERE IS MARCO?!" Bayu screamed, his face splattered with red light from the flare, tears streaming down his soot-covered cheeks.

We looked around the lobby. Marco was gone. His heavy audio recording bag was lying on the floor, the headphone cord snapped clean in half.

"MARCO!" we both screamed, running down the central corridor toward the rear courtyards.

The sound of the roaring voices above us had shifted. Now, a single, sharp bell was tolling from the top of the abandoned bell tower—CLANG... CLANG... CLANG...—even though the iron bell had been removed by the Italian government in the late 1970s.

We ran blindly through the ruined corridors, guided only by the sputtering light of Bayu's flare. We found Marco at the base of the bell tower courtyard.

He was kneeling in the dirt, his head buried in his hands, rocking back and forth violently. His fingers were digging so deep into the ash-rich soil that his fingernails were torn and bleeding. He was weeping hysterically, muttering incoherently in a language he didn't even speak.

"Marco! Get up! We have to go!" I roared, grabbing him under his armpits while Bayu grabbed his legs.

Marco fought us with frightening, unnatural strength, his eyes rolled completely back into his head, showing only milky whites. "The pit is full!" he shrieked, his voice cracking into a high-pitched scream. "The doctor says the blood cleans the ash! The doctor says we stay!"

We didn't argue. We dragged his limp, screaming body through the brambles, tearing our jackets and skin on the sharp thorns, running blindly toward the south canal inlet.

Behind us, the entire main building of the psychiatric hospital seemed to come alive. Lights—pale, sickly green lanterns—were flickering in every single shattered window of the three-story structure. Shadows were pacing back and forth behind the window frames, hundreds of them, staring down at us as we fled.

We reached the crumbling brick pier at 2:18 AM. We were twelve minutes early.

The lagoon was dead quiet, shrouded in thick, freezing fog. There was no sign of Paolo’s boat.

"PAOLO!" I screamed into the mist, my lungs burning, holding Marco down as he throngs and convulsed on the wet brickwork. "PAOLO, PLEASE!"

Behind us, at the tree line thirty yards away, the bushes parted.

Out stepped three tall, slender figures. They didn't walk; they drifted an inch above the powdery soil. They were shrouded in heavy, decaying black leather robes, wearing the iconic, long-beaked bird masks worn by 17th-century Plague Doctors. The glass lenses over their eye sockets were pitch black, reflecting no light.

They stood at the edge of the woods, staring at us in absolute silence, slowly raising three long, wooden canes in our direction.

I pulled my pocket knife out, holding it with shaking hands, standing in front of Bayu and Marco. "Stay back!" I screamed, my voice cracking with pathetic desperation. "Stay the fuck back!"

Just as the three masked figures took a step forward, the roar of a 200-horsepower marine engine shattered the silence of the lagoon.

Paolo’s wooden boat crashed through the fog, slamming into the pier with enough force to splinter the hull. Paolo was standing at the wheel, holding a heavy flare gun in one hand, his face white as a sheet.

"GET IN! GET IN NOW!" Paolo bellowed, firing a red star-shell flare over the tree line.

We hauled Marco over the gunwale, throwing our bodies onto the wooden floorboards. Bayu leaped aboard last, pushing off the pier with his boot. Paolo slammed the throttle to full power, the propeller screaming as it chewed through the shallow water.

As the boat surged away from the island into the open lagoon, I looked back over the stern.

Poveglia was receding into the mist. But standing on the crumbling edge of the brick dock, bathed in the eerie red light of the dying flare, were not just three plague doctors.

Dozens of pale, emaciated figures stood shoulder to shoulder along the shoreline, holding hands, watching us leave. And high up in the bell tower, a lone figure dressed in a white doctor's coat was leaning out over the stone ledge, slowly waving a gloved hand goodbye.

We flew back to Jakarta forty-eight hours later. We abandoned thousands of dollars worth of camera gear on that pier—we didn't care. We just wanted to get out of Europe.

Marco spent two weeks in a private psychiatric clinic in South Jakarta. He doesn't remember anything about that night, but he suffered permanent hearing damage in his left ear from the high-frequency static, and he refuses to be in a room with the lights off.

Bayu deleted his footage. He quit urban exploration entirely, sold his cameras, and moved back to his family's village in East Java. He doesn't answer my calls anymore.

As for me? I haven't slept a full night in almost a year.

Every time I shut my eyes, every time the room gets dark and quiet, I hear that smooth, cultured voice whispering in my left ear, vibrating directly inside my brain:

"Non c'è cura qui."

If you are an urban explorer, an influencer, or a ghost hunter looking for a thrill—I am begging you, listen to me. Do not bribe a captain in Venice. Do not pay to see Poveglia Island. Some places are not just haunted by memories. They are traps built out of human suffering, waiting for new souls to fill the pit.
