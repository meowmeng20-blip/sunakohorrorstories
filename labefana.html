LA BEFANA

Sender: Matteo Rossi • Italian Folklore

If you ask anyone in Rome, Florence, or Venice about La Befana, they’ll smile and tell you the same cozy story every child in Italy grows up hearing.

They’ll tell you she’s a kindly old woman—a harmless, soot-stained witch riding a broomstick on the night of January 5th. She enters through chimneys, slips sweet fruit, chocolates, and small wooden toys into the stockings of good children, and leaves a chunk of black sugar charcoal for the mischievous ones. It’s a sweet, festive fairy tale. Every year on Epiphany Eve, towns across the country set up brightly lit Christmas markets, sell stuffed rag dolls of her with crooked noses, and drink hot mulled wine.

I used to think it was just cute folklore too. I grew up in Florence, surrounded by renaissance art and modern city life. But my family’s roots trace back to a small, isolated village high in the Apennine Mountains of Tuscany—a place called San Pellegrino in Alpe. Up there, surrounded by dense pine forests, razor-sharp limestone peaks, and winter blizzards that cut off electricity for days, the old people don't sell rag dolls.

And they certainly don't smile when they talk about Epiphany Eve.

My name is Matteo. I’m thirty-eight years old, and what I’m about to write is something I’ve kept hidden in a locked wooden chest for nearly fifteen years. I’m sending this to you now because I can’t carry the weight of it alone anymore, and because the anniversary of my grandfather’s death is approaching.

To understand what happened to me, you have to understand my grandfather, Nonno Lorenzo.

Nonno Lorenzo was a stone mason who lived alone in an ancestral stone cottage built directly into the side of a steep mountain cliff outside San Pellegrino. He was a silent, imposing man whose hands looked like carved walnut wood. He rarely spoke, never went to church, and lived by strict, archaic rules that made no sense to my parents.

Every year, as December drew to a close, Nonno Lorenzo would perform a ritual that used to make my mother furious. He would haul heavy sacks of raw birch charcoal, blocks of tallow lard, dried bunches of mountain thyme, and heavy iron chains up to his cottage. While the rest of Italy celebrated Christmas and prepared for the fun of L'Epifania, Nonno Lorenzo boarded up his lower windows with thick oak planks.

In January of 2011, my grandfather suffered a severe stroke. He refused to go to a hospital in Florence, insisting on remaining in his mountain cottage. Since my parents were trapped in Rome due to work, the responsibility fell on me. I was twenty-three at the time, full of arrogance, completely convinced that country folk were just uneducated and superstitious. I drove my old Fiat Panda up the winding, icy mountain roads to nurse him through the first week of January.

I arrived on the afternoon of January 4th. The snow was already knee-deep, swirling in violent white sheets under a slate-gray sky.

The cottage felt like a fortress. The air inside smelled faintly of stale pipe tobacco, burning dried herbs, and old stone. Nonno Lorenzo lay in his heavy iron bed by the fireplace, his right side paralyzed, his eyes milky with fever and age. But despite his physical weakness, his mind was hyper-vigilant, his left hand gripping his wooden rosary with white knuckles.

The Ancient Offering:

San Pellegrino in Alpe

"Matteo," he rasped, his voice sounding like two dry stones grinding together. "Listen to me closely. Tomorrow is the fifth."

"I know, Nonno," I said, putting my jacket on a hook and stoking the fire. "January 5th. Epiphany Eve. The kids in town are probably getting their candy ready."

He spat into the hearth, the fire hissing sharply. "Idiots in the valley. They think she is a saint. They think she brings sugar. They forgot what the ancients traded her for."

I sighed, pulling up a chair. "Nonno, please. Don't start with the old stories. You need to rest."

"Shut your mouth and listen!" he growled, his left eye widening with an intensity that chilled me. "She is not a giver of gifts, Matteo. She is an ancient collector. Before the priests came, before the Romans built their roads, She walked these mountains. She takes the rot out of the house. She demands the black charcoal to burn the year’s sins, and she claims what is owed from those who do not pay."

He reached under his heavy wool blanket, pulled out a heavy, tarnished brass key on a leather strap, and pressed it into my palm.

"Tomorrow night, before the sun drops behind the ridge, you must prepare the doorstep," he instructed, his breathing heavy and ragged. "A bowl of goat’s milk, mixed with salt and ash. Three lumps of pure birch charcoal placed on the stone sill. A bundle of dried thyme tied with red twine hung above the lintel. And whatever you do... when the wind turns north at midnight, do NOT open the door. Do not look through the shutters. No matter who calls your name. No matter what voice you hear."

I took the key just to appease him. "Okay, Nonno. I’ll do it."

"Swear it on your mother’s soul," he demanded, his fingers digging painfully into my wrist. "Swear it, Matteo."

"I swear," I whispered.

The next day, January 5th, a brutal blizzard blew in from the north, trapping us completely. The power lines collapsed around 3:00 PM, plunging the cottage into darkness save for the orange glow of the hearth and a few flickering beeswax candles. The wind howled through the ravine like a choir of dying animals, rattling the thick timber rafters overhead.

By 6:00 PM, darkness had completely swallowed the mountains.

I set about making dinner for my grandfather, but his condition had worsened. He lay staring at the heavy oak front door, his breathing shallow, sweating despite the freezing draft seeping through the stone floorboards.

Around 9:00 PM, I remembered his strange instructions. Feeling foolish, but wanting to honor his wishes, I went to the pantry. I found the bundle of dried thyme, the red twine, and the bowl of goat's milk. But when I searched the cellar for the birch charcoal, I realized with a sinking feeling that the sack was empty—nothing left but useless, fine gray dust.

"It doesn't matter," I muttered to myself, rubbing my tired eyes. "It's just an old superstition anyway."

I hung the thyme above the door and placed the bowl of salted milk on the outdoor stone threshold, stepping out briefly into the blinding white noise of the blizzard. The cold air felt like needles against my face. The forest surrounding the cottage was pitch black, the massive pines swaying violently under the gale.

I went back inside, bolted the heavy iron latch on the oak door, and sat by my grandfather's bed.

Around 11:30 PM, the storm suddenly died down.

Midnight Approaching:

The Knock at the Door

The roaring wind stopped completely, replaced by an eerie, suffocating silence that made my ears ring. The heavy snowfall ceased, leaving the air outside crystal clear and freezing cold. Through the small, barred window in the loft, the full moon cast a sharp, pale blue light across the cabin floor.

Nonno Lorenzo's eyes snapped open. He couldn't speak anymore, but his body was rigid with terror.

Then, I heard it.

It started as a faint, rhythmic sound out on the frozen snowpack. Scritch... scritch... scritch...

It sounded like someone dragging a heavy bundle of dry, thorny sticks across the ice. It was moving slowly up the steep stone path toward the cottage.

My heart began to hammer violently against my ribs. I sat frozen in my chair, staring at the front door.

Scritch... scritch... scritch...

The sound stopped right outside our threshold.

For two long, agonizing minutes, there was absolute silence. I held my breath, the smell of ozone and sharp, bitter woodsmoke suddenly filling the sealed room, despite the hearth fire having died down to embers.

Then came a knock.

It wasn't a metallic bang or a polite tap. It sounded like thick, heavy bone striking the solid oak wood—three distinct, agonizingly slow blows. THUD. THUD. THUD.

My grandfather let out a wet, strangled gasp from his bed, his knuckles turning white as he gripped his sheets.

A voice called out from the other side of the door.

It was a voice that didn't sound human. It was dry, ancient, and raspy, sounding like dry leaves blowing across a graveyard floor, yet it carried an unnatural, hypnotic resonance that seemed to vibrate directly inside my skull.

"Lorenzo..." the voice creaked, dragging out the syllables. "The year has turned. The hearth is cold. Show me the black stone. Pay the price of the roof."

I couldn't move. My tongue felt glued to the roof of my mouth.

I heard the wet, slubbering sound of something kneeling down on the snow outside the threshold. The bowl of salted milk clinked against the stone.

"The milk is offered..." the dry voice whispered, right up against the crack of the door. "The herb is hung... But where is the stone, Lorenzo? Where is the black fuel for my sack?"

A heavy, scraping breath rattled through the wood.

"You gave me dust... You send me away empty..."

Suddenly, the tone of the voice shifted. The frail, creaking tone vanished, replaced by an icy, malicious fury that made the blood in my veins turn to frost.

"There is an unburned soul in this house."

The iron latch on the heavy oak door began to shake violently. The thick wood groaned under a immense, crushing pressure from the outside, as if a giant fell tree were leaning against it. Dark, black soot began to pour through the keyhole, spiraling into the room like ink dissolving in water.

"Matteo..." Nonno Lorenzo managed to choke out, his left hand pointing frantically toward the floorboards beneath his bed. "The... the iron!"

I snapped out of my paralysis. I dove beneath his iron bedframe, my hands frantically searching the dusty stone floor until my fingers hit a cold, iron ring set into a small wooden trapdoor—a root cellar I didn't even know existed.

Outside, the door began to splinter. Heavy, claw-like scraping sounds tore through the thick oak planks. I could hear something pulling itself up the stone steps—a dry, rustling movement, accompanied by the distinct, sickening smell of wet grave soil, burning hair, and bitter ash.

I hauled the trapdoor open. Inside was a small, stone-lined pit containing an ancient, rust-covered iron box with a massive padlock.

I remembered the tarnished brass key my grandfather had given me the day before.

With shaking hands, I pulled the key from my pocket and jammed it into the rusty lock. It resisted for a terrifying second before turning with a sharp CLACK.

I threw open the lid of the iron box.

Inside lay three dense, pitch-black blocks of hand-carved birch charcoal, wrapped in yellowed parchment inscribed with ancient Latin prayers, alongside a small copper bowl filled with dried blood.

I didn't care about the prayers or the blood. I grabbed the three blocks of charcoal, scrambled out from under the bed, and ran straight toward the front door.

The central plank of the oak door had just cracked down the middle.

The True Face:

The Ancient Collector

Through the narrow, splintered gap, in the pale blue moonlight, I saw it.

It was not a sweet old woman.

A tall, horribly gaunt figure stooped low against the porch. She was shrouded in thick, filthy rags of dark burlap that seemed to merge with the shadows themselves. Her skin was pulled taut over a skeletal frame, dark as charred wood, glistening with oil and soot. Her fingers were impossibly long, ending in thick, curved yellow nails that were currently gouging deep grooves into the oak door.

And her face—God help me, her face—was an oval of nightmares. Her nose was a hooked beak of cartilage, her mouth a wide, lipless tear filled with jagged, blackened teeth. But her eyes... she had no eyes. Where her eye sockets should have been, two tiny, brilliant embers of red coal burned softly in the dark, staring straight through the wood into my soul.

She smiled at me through the crack. It was a wide, sickening grin that split her blackened face almost to the ears.

"Give it to me, boy..." she hissed, extending one long, soot-covered arm through the shattered plank. Her clawed fingers reached for my throat, cold air radiating off her skin like ice from a deep cavern.

With a scream of sheer, primal terror, I didn't reach out to her. Instead, I shoved the three blocks of birch charcoal directly through the broken gap, slamming them hard into her outstretched, clawed hand.

The moment her skin touched the charcoal, a blinding white flash of light erupted on the porch.

A sound filled the air—not a scream of pain, but a deep, resonant sigh of profound, terrifying satisfaction, like a roaring fire suddenly catching wind in a deep chimney. The bitter smell of ozone swept through the crack, instantly suffocating the stench of rot and ash.

The pressure against the door vanished instantly.

I fell backward onto the stone floor, gasping for air, my heart beating so fast I thought my chest would burst.

Silence returned to the mountain. A deep, heavy, absolute silence.

I lay on that floor for two hours, unable to move, holding an iron fireplace poker tightly in my hand. When the first rays of dawn finally pierced through the loft window at 7:00 AM, illuminating the room with pale winter light, I slowly forced myself to stand up.

My grandfather lay motionless in his bed. He had passed away quietly sometime during the night, his face finally peaceful, his hands resting on his chest.

With trembling legs, I walked over to the front door.

The heavy oak planks were deeply scarred, with four-inch grooves gouged clean through the wood. The bowl of goat's milk on the threshold was completely empty, wiped clean as if washed with water.

And on the top stone step, resting right where I had dropped them, were three perfectly shaped pieces of pure, sweet white sugar, sparkling brightly in the morning sun.

I buried my grandfather two days later in the churchyard at San Pellegrino. Shortly after the funeral, I sold the mountain cottage to a local timber company on the strict condition that the structure be torn down completely. I never went back to those mountains. I moved away from Florence, eventually leaving Italy altogether.

I know how crazy this sounds. I know that if you show this to anyone, they'll tell you it was a nightmarish hallucination brought on by cold, exhaustion, and grief over my dying grandfather.

But every year, when January 5th arrives, I lock my apartment doors. I close all the blinds. I light three candles, and I place three pieces of pure birch charcoal on my windowsill. Because I know that out there, beyond the bright city lights and the festive Christmas markets selling cute rag dolls, She is still walking the cold mountain passes. And if you leave her hearth empty... she will always come inside to collect.
